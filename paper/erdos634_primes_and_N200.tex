\begin{abstract}
A positive integer $N$ is a \emph{tile count} if some triangle can be cut into $N$ pairwise congruent
triangles; Erd\H{o}s asked for the set of tile counts. The problem is not solved here. The results below
are theorems of an accompanying Lean~4 library unless stated otherwise.
\textup{(1)} A prime $p$ is a tile count if and only if $p=3$ or $p\not\equiv3\pmod4$. This is a theorem of
the formal library, with the standard axioms; the formal statement has not yet been compared with the
mathematical one by an independent reader.
\textup{(2)} For $1\le N\le200$ the tile counts are determined; the largest search certificate, which excludes $135$, has
$1\,326\,256$ nodes and is checked by the Lean kernel.
\textup{(3)} For a tile with angles $(\alpha,\beta,2\pi/3)$, $\alpha/\pi$ irrational, a signed-direction
functional on directed edges gives two integrality conditions; no prime number of such tiles tiles an
isosceles, non-equilateral triangle.
\textup{(4)} For coprime $1\le e<f$ let $T_M(e,f)$ be the isosceles triangle with legs $Mf^3$ and base
$Me(3f^2-e^2)$, with the tile $(ef,f^2-e^2,f^2)$. A tiling exists for every $M\ge f$ (constructions of
D.~Paliy and M.~Beeson, formalized here); there is none for $M=1$, none when $2Me<f$, and for $e=1$ none when
$2M\le f$. The scales $M\ge2$ with $f/(2e)<M<f$ are open, apart from three instances; the open part is stated
precisely.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{The problem}
Let $T$ be a triangle. A triangle $\Delta$ is \emph{cut into $N$ copies of $T$}, or \emph{tiled by $N$
copies of $T$}, if $\Delta$ is the union of $N$ closed triangles with pairwise disjoint interiors, each
congruent to $T$. Mirror images of $T$ are allowed, and the pieces need not meet edge to edge: a vertex of
one piece may lie in the relative interior of a side of another. We call $\Delta$ the \emph{target} and $T$
the \emph{tile}, and we say that $N$ is a \emph{tile count} if some triangle can be cut into $N$ copies of
some triangle. Erd\H{o}s asked for the set of tile counts; see Soifer~\cite{Soifer}. It is Problem~634 of
the collection at \texttt{erdosproblems.com}, where $N=19$ is listed as an open instance.

Every square is a tile count, for any triangle, and so are the numbers $x^2+y^2$, $3k^2$ and $6k^2$
\cite{Soifer}. Laczkovich \cite{Laczkovich1995,Laczkovich2012} determined which pairs of shapes
$(\Delta,T)$ can occur, and Beeson examined the resulting classes one at a time
\cite{BeesonI,BeesonIII,BeesonIso,BeesonSeven,BeesonEq}. Zhang \cite{Zhang} constructed families of tilings
by tiles with an angle $2\pi/3$, and Beeson, Laczkovich and Zhang settled the neighbouring Problem~633
\cite{Erdos633}. Among the primes, $2$, $3$ and the primes $\equiv1\pmod4$ are tile counts by the classical
constructions. The primes $p\equiv3\pmod4$ with $p>3$ are the odd primes that are neither sums of two
squares nor of the form $3k^2$, and it is a folklore conjecture that none of them is a tile count. Beeson
proved this for $p=7$ \cite{BeesonSeven}.

The paper is accompanied by a library for the proof assistant Lean~4 with Mathlib, in the same repository;
Appendix~\ref{sec:formal} describes it and maps each numbered statement to its Lean declaration.
Problem~634 is not solved here. Section~\ref{sec:open} states what a solution still requires.

\subsection{Results}
One family of pairs $(\Delta,T)$ needs notation from the start (Definition~\ref{def:TW}). For coprime integers
$1\le e<f$ the triangle with sides $(a,b,c)=(ef,\,f^2-e^2,\,f^2)$ has angles $(\alpha,\beta,2\alpha+\beta)$
with $3\alpha+2\beta=\pi$ and $\alpha/\pi$ irrational. For an integer $M\ge1$ let $T_M(e,f)$ be the
isosceles triangle with legs $Mf^3$ and base $Me(3f^2-e^2)$; its base angles are $\beta$ and its apex is
$3\alpha$. Let $W_M(e,f)$ be the triangle with sides $Mf(f^2-e^2)$, $Me(2f^2-e^2)$, $Mf^3$; its angles are
$\beta$, $2\alpha$, $\alpha+\beta$. Their areas are $M^2(3f^2-e^2)$ and $M^2(2f^2-e^2)$ times the area of
the tile. We call $(e,f)$ a \emph{member}, $M$ the \emph{scale}, and the triangles $T_M(e,f)$ the
\emph{base-$\beta$ targets}.

Each numbered theorem, proposition, lemma, corollary and computation of this paper carries a mark \sF,
\sW{} or \sS, explained in Section~\ref{sub:standing}: \sF{} for a theorem of the Lean library, \sW{} for a
written proof that is not formalized, \sS{} for the outcome of a computer search without a certificate.

\begin{theorem}[Prime counts; \sF]\label{thm:primes}
Let $p$ be a prime. Some triangle can be cut into $p$ congruent triangles if and only if $p=3$ or
$p\not\equiv3\pmod4$.
\end{theorem}

\noindent\emph{Standing of Theorem~\ref{thm:primes}.} The theorem is formalized in Lean~4 with Mathlib and
depends on the standard axioms only. The formal statement and the definitions it uses have been compared with
the mathematical ones within this work, and not yet by an independent reader. With this qualification, it says that no prime $p\equiv3\pmod4$ with $p>3$ is a tile count, and in particular that no triangle can be
cut into $19$ congruent triangles. The formal statement is displayed in Section~\ref{sub:formalprime}.

\noindent\emph{Priority.} A proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:primes} was first made public by D.~Paliy on 29
September 2026 \cite{PaliyPrime}, by a column induction from a corner of the scale-one targets. The proof given
here was obtained independently, by different arguments at scale one (Section~\ref{sub:scaleone}); its
exclusion of the family $e=1$ was public on 12 September 2026 \cite{Zenodo32}, and the rest was completed
without being made public at the time.

\medskip
The formal proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:primes} is a case analysis on the tile. Arithmetic conditions close
every case except three targets of the tile $(ef,f^2-e^2,f^2)$: the base-$\beta$ target $T_1(e,f)$, with $3f^2-e^2$
tiles, the target $W_1(e,f)$, with $2f^2-e^2$ tiles, and one isosceles target. The last two lead back to the
first (Section~\ref{sub:cases}). The primes of the form $3f^2-e^2$ are the primes $\equiv11\pmod{12}$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:mod12}). For these three targets the theorem rests on the following statement,
which concerns every member and any number of tiles.

\begin{theorem}[The base-$\beta$ targets at scale one; \sF]\label{thm:scaleone}
Let $1\le e<f$ be coprime integers. No triangle with sides $f^3$, $f^3$, $e(3f^2-e^2)$ can be cut into
congruent triangles with sides $ef$, $f^2-e^2$, $f^2$, in any number.
\end{theorem}

The standing of Theorem~\ref{thm:scaleone} is that of Theorem~\ref{thm:primes}.

\begin{theorem}[Isosceles targets of a tile with an angle $2\pi/3$; \sF]\label{thm:iso}
Let $T$ have angles $(\alpha,\beta,2\pi/3)$ with $\alpha/\pi$ irrational. Then no prime number of copies of
$T$ tiles an isosceles triangle that is not equilateral.
\end{theorem}

For this target Beeson \cite{BeesonIso} showed that a tiling needs at least $36$ tiles and left the prime
case open. The statement of Theorem~\ref{thm:iso} is also proved by Beeson, by a different argument
\cite[Thms.~8 and~10]{BeesonNoPrime}; the proof given here uses the signed-direction functional of
Section~\ref{sec:invariant}.

\begin{theorem}[Tilings at every scale $M\ge f$; D.~Paliy, M.~Beeson; \sF]\label{thm:existence}
Let $1\le e<f$ be coprime integers and $M\ge f$. Then $W_M(e,f)$ and $T_M(e,f)$ can be cut into congruent
triangles with sides $(ef,\,f^2-e^2,\,f^2)$.
\end{theorem}

The statement and the constructions are not ours. The statement is announced by Paliy \cite{PaliyLedger};
the tiling at $M=f$ is a construction that his notes credit to Beeson \cite[Thm.~11]{BeesonIII}, and the
passage to all $M\ge f$ is by his exterior collar and his additional seeds. What is added here is a formal
proof for all members and scales.

\begin{theorem}[Runs on a side, members $(1,f)$; \sF]\label{thm:siderun}
Let $f\ge2$ be an integer and $(a,b,c)=(f,\,f^2-1,\,f^2)$, with angles $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ opposite
$a,b,c$. Let $\Delta$ be a triangle with a side $s$ whose two corners have the angles $\beta,\beta$ or
$\alpha+\beta,\beta$, and suppose that $\Delta$ is cut into copies of the triangle $(a,b,c)$. Then among the
tiles with an edge on $s$ there are $f$ consecutive ones that lay their side $c$ on $s$ and show the angle
$\alpha$ at the same end of it. These $f$ tiles are translates of one another, and $|s|\ge f^3$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}[Members $(1,f)$ below the scale $f$; \sF]\label{cor:below}
Let $f\ge2$ and $M\ge1$ be integers.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(a)}] $W_M(1,f)$ can be cut into copies of $(f,f^2-1,f^2)$ if and only if $M\ge f$.
\item[\textup{(b)}] If $2M\le f$, then $T_M(1,f)$ cannot be cut into copies of $(f,f^2-1,f^2)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

The next three statements extend Theorem~\ref{thm:siderun} and part of Corollary~\ref{cor:below} from the
members $(1,f)$ to every member. All three are formal.

\begin{theorem}[Runs on a side, every member; \sF]\label{thm:siderunall}
Let $1\le e<f$ be coprime integers and $(a,b,c)=(ef,\,f^2-e^2,\,f^2)$, with angles $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$
opposite $a,b,c$. Let $\Delta$ be a triangle with a side $s$ whose two corners have the angles $\beta,\beta$
or $\alpha+\beta,\beta$, and suppose that $\Delta$ is cut into copies of the triangle $(a,b,c)$. Then among
the tiles with an edge on $s$ there are $f$ consecutive ones that lay their side $c$ on $s$ and show the
angle $\alpha$ at the same end of it. In particular $|s|\ge f^3$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}[Every member below the scale $f$: two sides; \sF]\label{cor:ebelow}
Let $1\le e<f$ be coprime integers, $(a,b,c)=(ef,\,f^2-e^2,\,f^2)$, and $M\ge1$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(a)}] If $Me<f$, then $W_M(e,f)$ cannot be cut into copies of $(a,b,c)$.
\item[\textup{(b)}] If $2Me<f$, then $T_M(e,f)$ cannot be cut into copies of $(a,b,c)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

\begin{corollary}[Every member below the scale $f$: the third side of $W_M(e,f)$; \sF]\label{cor:thickw}
Let $1\le e<f$ be coprime integers, $(a,b,c)=(ef,\,f^2-e^2,\,f^2)$, and $M\ge1$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(a)}] If $M(f-e)\le e$, then $W_M(e,f)$ cannot be cut into copies of $(a,b,c)$.
\item[\textup{(b)}] For every $f\ge2$, the triangle $W_M(f-1,f)$ can be cut into copies of
$(f(f-1),\,2f-1,\,f^2)$ if and only if $M\ge f$.
\item[\textup{(c)}] The triangle $W_2(3,5)$, with sides $160$, $246$, $250$, cannot be cut into copies of
$(15,16,25)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

In part \textup{(b)} of Corollary~\ref{cor:thickw} the tilings for $M\ge f$ are those of
Theorem~\ref{thm:existence}; the exclusion for $M<f$ comes from part \textup{(a)}. Both halves are formal.
For $e\ge2$ the inequality $2Me\le f$ is the same as $2Me<f$, since $e$ and $f$ are coprime; so part
\textup{(b)} of Corollary~\ref{cor:ebelow} is the counterpart of Corollary~\ref{cor:below}\textup{(b)}.

\begin{proposition}[A layer of $T_M(1,f)$; \sF]\label{prop:layer}
Let $f\ge2$ be an integer.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(a)}] If $M\ge1$, $2M\ge f$, and $T_M(1,f)$ has a tiling by the tile $(f,f^2-1,f^2)$, so has
$T_{M+1}(1,f)$.
\item[\textup{(b)}] There is an integer $M^*(f)$ with $f/2<M^*(f)\le f$ such that, for $M\ge1$, $T_M(1,f)$ has a
tiling if and only if $M\ge M^*(f)$.
\item[\textup{(c)}] $M^*(f)=f$, that is, $\tau(1,f,M)$ holds for every $M\ge1$, if and only if $T_{f-1}(1,f)$ has
no tiling.
\end{enumerate}
The tiling of \textup{(a)} is explicit: for $2M'\ge f+2$ the trapezoid cut from $T_{M'}(1,f)$ by the line parallel
to the base at the height $H_1$ of $T_1(1,f)$ is cut into $(2M'-1)(3f^2-1)$ copies of the tile (the \emph{layer}),
and the part above it is a translate of $T_{M'-1}(1,f)$.
\end{proposition}

Proposition~\ref{prop:layer} is a theorem of the Lean library in the form \textup{(a)}--\textup{(c)}; the
description of the layer is the construction of its proof.

In every case decided so far $T_M(e,f)$ has a tiling exactly when $M\ge f$. Whether this is the rule is
open:

\begin{question}[The scales below $f$]\label{q:threshold}
Let $1\le e<f$ be coprime and $2\le M<f$. Can $T_M(e,f)$ be cut into copies of $(ef,f^2-e^2,f^2)$?
\end{question}

The answer is no for $(e,f,M)=(2,3,2)$ and $(3,4,2)$, for $e=1$ and $2M\le f$, and for $(1,3,2)$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:scale2}, Corollary~\ref{cor:below}); these answers are formal. By
Corollary~\ref{cor:ebelow}\textup{(b)}, which is formal as well, the answer is no whenever $2Me<f$;
for $e\ge2$ and $M\ge2$ this requires $f>4e$, and the first such case is $T_2(2,9)$. The open cases are
therefore those with $f/(2e)<M<f$ other than $(1,3,2)$, $(2,3,2)$ and $(3,4,2)$. For $e=1$ these are
$f/2<M<f$ with $f\ge4$, and by Proposition~\ref{prop:layer} they are governed by the single case $M=f-1$: if
$T_{f-1}(1,f)$ has no tiling, neither has $T_M(1,f)$ for any $M<f$. The first such cases are $T_3(1,4)$,
with $423$ tiles, and $T_4(1,5)$, with $1184$. For $e\ge2$ the first open cases are $T_3(3,4)$, with $351$
tiles, and the scales $2\le M\le4$ of the members $(2,5)$, $(3,5)$, $(4,5)$; for a member with $2e>f$ every
scale $2\le M<f$ is open, apart from $(2,3,2)$ and $(3,4,2)$. For $e\ge2$ a layer exists as well, of $e$ times
the height of $T_1(e,f)$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:layerall}, formal); for the members $(f-1,f)$ it exists
only at the scales $M\ge f$ and does not reduce the open scales. We do not know the answer in any open case.

For the triangles $W_M(e,f)$ more is known. They are settled for the members $(1,f)$
(Corollary~\ref{cor:below}) and for the members $(f-1,f)$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:thickw}), both formal: a
tiling exists exactly when $M\ge f$. For the other members there is none when $Me<f$ or
$M(f-e)\le e$, and the scales $M<f$ outside these two ranges are open, apart from $W_2(3,5)$; the first are
$W_3(2,5)$, $W_4(2,5)$, $W_3(3,5)$ and $W_4(3,5)$. By Proposition~\ref{prop:wstrip}, which is formal, the open
scales of a member are governed by the single scale $M=f-1$: if $W_{f-1}(e,f)$ has no tiling,
neither has $W_M(e,f)$ for any $M<f$.

Further results are stated in the sections: the commensurability of the sides of a tile with an angle
$\pi/3$ or $2\pi/3$ that tiles an equilateral triangle, which are theorems of Laczkovich and of Beeson and
Zhang formalized here (Theorem~\ref{thm:relations}); statements for infinitely many composite $N$
(Section~\ref{sub:infinite}); and the members for which Question~\ref{q:threshold} is answered at every
scale (Proposition~\ref{prop:finite}).

\subsection{Ideas of the proofs}\label{sub:ideas}

\emph{Theorem~\ref{thm:iso}.} For a tile with an angle $2\pi/3$ one would like to colour the tiles in two
colours and count. This is not available, because a vertex of a tiling may be surrounded by an even number
of tiles. We weight directed edges instead. Every edge of a tiling has a direction $j\frac\pi3+k\alpha$ with
integers $j,k$, and $(-1)^j$ depends only on the direction because $\alpha/\pi$ is irrational; the weight of
a directed edge is its length times $(-1)^j$. The weight changes sign when the edge is reversed, so the two
sides of an interior segment cancel, and it is linear in the length, so the cancellation does not depend on
how the segment is subdivided on either side. Around a tile the weights add up to $\pm(c+a-b)$. The move
exchanges a sum over the $N$ tiles, about which nothing is known, for the weight of the boundary of the
target, which is explicit: the latter is an integer multiple of $c+a-b$, and the integer has the parity of
$N$. For an isosceles target and prime $N$ the integer would be $(c-a-b)/\sqrt b$, with a primitive integer
triple $c^2=a^2+ab+b^2$, and this is never an integer.

\emph{Theorem~\ref{thm:primes}.} The tile is sorted into finitely many types by its angles
(Proposition~\ref{prop:types}), and for each type the area and the two boundary weights give equations
between integers. For a prime $N\equiv3\pmod4$, $N>3$, these equations have no solution in any case but
one. In the remaining case the tile is $(ef,f^2-e^2,f^2)$, and the target is $T_1(e,f)$, or it is $W_1(e,f)$
or an isosceles triangle with base angles $\alpha+\beta$, and in the last two cases gluing one or two scaled
tiles to the target produces a tiling of $T_1(e,f)$. So a statement about all triangles and all tiles is
exchanged for the absence of tilings of one explicit two-parameter family, Theorem~\ref{thm:scaleone}. The
exchange is legitimate because the list of types is complete and because a prime number of tiles forces the
scale to be one.

\emph{Theorem~\ref{thm:scaleone}.} No count can prove it. The same tile tiles $T_M(e,f)$ for all $M\ge f$,
and a quantity that depends on the scale periodically or polynomially takes at scale one the value it takes
at those scales (Proposition~\ref{prop:blind}). The proof uses the order of the tiles: which tile lies next
to which. Each side of the target is a word in the letters $a,b,c$, and the length of the side leaves few
words. At a point of a side the angles of the tiles add up to $\pi$, which with $\alpha/\pi$ irrational
leaves two figures, and this orients the tiles of the word one after the other. The step that carries the
argument into the interior is a partition lemma (Lemma~\ref{lem:partition}): a segment that is a union of
edges on one side and is closed off at both ends is a union of whole edges on the other side as well. Since
$b$ is not a sum of other edges, and $kb$ for $k<f$ is a sum of edges in one way only, a tile laid on such a
segment forces the tile across it. One follows the forced tiles from a corner until a point is reached where
no figure is possible. For the members $(1,f)$, $f\ge3$, the walk runs along the base; for the others it
starts at the apex.

\emph{Theorem~\ref{thm:200}.} For a given $N$ the classification and the arithmetic leave finitely many
pairs $(\Delta,T)$ with explicit sides. Most of them are tiled by a known construction or fail a further
arithmetic condition. Each of the others is decided by an exhaustive search, and the statement ``no tiling
exists'' is exchanged for ``this finite tree is a complete case distinction and every leaf is dead''. The
tree branches at a convex corner of the region not yet covered, where a tile of any tiling must have a
corner, with an edge along the boundary: six placements. That these six cases are exhaustive is a theorem of
the library, and each node of the tree, written in exact arithmetic over a real quadratic field, is checked
by the Lean kernel. The search program that found the tree is not trusted and its floating-point verdict is
not used.

\emph{Theorem~\ref{thm:existence}.} The tilings are explicit layouts of scaled tiles and of parallelograms
made of two tiles, written in one skew coordinate system in which all vertices are integer points. The
verification of a layout is exchanged for three finite checks: each piece lies in the target, any two pieces
are separated by a line, and the numbers of tiles add up to the number that the area requires. The third
check replaces the proof that the pieces cover the target, since pieces with disjoint interiors inside the
target whose areas add up to its area leave no open set uncovered.

\emph{Theorem~\ref{thm:siderun} and Corollary~\ref{cor:below}.} Let $k<f$ equal tiles lie side by side with
their sides $c$ on $s$. Above them the tile lattice is forced row by row: each row of the lattice is a
segment closed off at both ends, the partition lemma applies, and $jb$ has one representation for $j<f$. The
induction ends at a chord of the lattice triangle that cannot be covered, so a run of fewer than $f$ such
tiles cannot be bounded on both sides in the ways that the angles at the ends of $s$ allow. A count of the
possible neighbours of a run then shows that some run has length at least $f$. For a run of exactly $f$
tiles the induction stops at its last chord, because $fb=(f^2-1)a$ has a second representation; this is what
the tilings of Theorem~\ref{thm:existence} show. The corollary follows by counting edges on a side modulo
$f$ (Section~\ref{sub:below}).

\emph{Theorem~\ref{thm:siderunall} and Corollaries~\ref{cor:ebelow} and~\ref{cor:thickw}.} The proof for
$e=1$ uses two properties of $e=1$, and the proof for all members replaces both. The first is an
inequality between sides. The lattice triangle above a run of $k<f$ tiles is
bounded on the right by the line through the right end of the run along the side $a$ of the last tile, and
the induction needs that every lattice point on this line is passed by a tile lying beyond the line. For
$e=1$ this is immediate: the tile that closes the run on the right lays an edge $b$ or $c$ on that line, the
lattice points on it are at distances $a,2a,\dots,ka$ from the run, and $ka<b$, so this one edge passes all
of them. For $e\ge2$ the inequality fails: $3a>b$ for the tile $(10,21,25)$, and $a>b$ for $(6,5,9)$. The
general proof replaces the one long edge by the partition lemma applied to that line. The segment from the
right end of the run to a lattice point is a union of $j<f$ edges $a$ of lattice tiles; it is clamped at its
lower end; and $ja$ is a sum of edges only as $a+\dots+a$ when $j<f$. So if no tile beyond the line passed
the lattice point, the edge $b$ or $c$ of the closing tile would be one of $j$ edges $a$. The cost is that
the clamping at the lower end has to be carried through the induction as part of its hypothesis; at each
step it is supplied by the side $s$ or by an edge of a lattice tile already placed. The second property
concerns angles: where the formal proof for $e=1$ uses the inequality $3\alpha<\beta$, which is false for
$(6,5,9)$, the general proof uses only the list of the ways in which angles of tiles fill a given angle,
which is the same for every member (Lemma~\ref{lem:figures}). Corollary~\ref{cor:ebelow} then follows by the
count modulo $f$ used for $e=1$. Corollary~\ref{cor:thickw} concerns the side of $W_M(e,f)$ that starts at
the corner $2\alpha$. There the tile beside the first tile of a run can be its mirror image, a case that
does not arise at a corner $\beta$ or $\alpha+\beta$. A second induction over the same lattice excludes it
for runs of at most $e$ tiles. The bound $e$ comes from the relation $ec=fa$: a row of $e$ lattice edges $c$
can also be covered by $f$ edges $a$, and the induction does not control that covering
(Section~\ref{sub:allmembers}).

\section{Definitions and the classification}\label{sec:defs}

\subsection{Dissections}

\begin{definition}\label{def:tiling}
A \emph{triangle} is the convex hull of three affinely independent points of the plane. A \emph{dissection}
of a triangle $\Delta$ into $N$ triangles is a family $t_1,\dots,t_N$ of triangles with
$t_1\cup\dots\cup t_N=\Delta$ whose interiors are pairwise disjoint. It is a dissection \emph{into $N$
copies of $T$}, or an \emph{$N$-tiling of $\Delta$ by $T$}, if every $t_i$ is the image of $T$ under an
isometry of the plane. A positive integer $N$ is a \emph{tile count} if such a dissection exists for some
$\Delta$ and some $T$.
\end{definition}

Isometries include reflections, and nothing is assumed on how the tiles meet. These are the definitions of
the Lean library (Section~\ref{sub:leandefs}). The sides of the tile are $a,b,c$ and the opposite angles
$\alpha,\beta,\gamma$. An edge of a tile of length $a$ is an \emph{$a$-edge}, and a tile \emph{shows} the
angle $\alpha$ at a point if the point is its vertex of angle $\alpha$; likewise for the other letters. An
edge of length $a$ joins the vertices of angles $\beta$ and $\gamma$.

\begin{proposition}[Dissection basics; \sF]\label{prop:basics}
Let $t_1,\dots,t_N$ be a dissection of $\Delta$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(i)}] $\Area(\Delta)=\sum_i\Area(t_i)$; for $N$ copies of $T$, $\Area(\Delta)=N\Area(T)$.
\item[\textup{(ii)}] At a point $v$ of $\Delta$ the angles of the tiles containing $v$ add up to $2\pi$ if
$v$ is interior to $\Delta$, to $\pi$ if $v$ is in the relative interior of a side, and to the angle of
$\Delta$ if $v$ is a corner. A tile contributes its angle at $v$ if $v$ is one of its vertices and $\pi$ if
$v$ lies in the relative interior of one of its edges.
\item[\textup{(iii)}] Each side of $\Delta$ is the union of the edges of tiles that lie on it, and these
edges follow one another along the side without overlap.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

By (iii) a side of the target of length $L$, tiled by a tile with sides $a,b,c$, gives nonnegative integers
with $n_a a+n_b b+n_c c=L$; the sequence of the letters along the side is its \emph{word}. The next lemma is
the interior version of (iii).

\begin{lemma}[Partition lemma; \sF]\label{lem:partition}
In a dissection of $\Delta$, let $[P,Q]$ be a segment whose open part lies in the interior of $\Delta$ and
contains no interior point of a tile, and let $\sigma$ be one of the two sides of its line. Suppose that no
tile lying on the side $\sigma$ has an edge on the line that contains $P$ or $Q$ in its relative interior.
Then the edges on the line of the tiles on the side $\sigma$ that meet the open segment are contained in
$[P,Q]$, and their lengths add up to $|PQ|$.
\end{lemma}

The hypothesis at an end $P$ holds, in particular, when the line just beyond $P$ leaves $\Delta$ or enters
the interior of a tile; we then say that the segment is \emph{clamped} at $P$.

\subsection{Classical families}

\begin{proposition}[Classical families; \sF]\label{prop:families}
If a triangle $\Delta$ is cut into $N$ copies of $T$, then for every $k\ge1$ the triangle $k\Delta$ is cut
into $k^2N$ copies of $T$. The numbers $k^2$, $3k^2$, $6k^2$ and $x^2+y^2$ with $k,x,y\ge1$ are tile counts.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The lines parallel to the sides cut $k\Delta$ into $k^2$ triangles congruent to $\Delta$. A triangle is a
dissection of itself; an equilateral triangle is cut into three congruent triangles from its centre and
into six by its medians; and the altitude of a right triangle with legs $x$, $y$ cuts it into two triangles
similar to it with ratios $x/\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$ and $y/\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$, which are cut into $x^2$ and $y^2$
congruent triangles.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The types of tiles}\label{sub:types}
The pairs $(\Delta,T)$ for which a tiling can exist were classified by Laczkovich
\cite{Laczkovich1995,Laczkovich2012}. We use the classification in the organisation of Beeson
\cite{BeesonSeven}. If all angles of $T$ are rational multiples of $\pi$, then $N$ is a square, a sum of two
squares, or $2$, $3$ or $6$ times a square \cite[Thm.~3 and Table~3]{BeesonSeven}. If some angle of $T$ is
not a rational multiple of $\pi$, and $\Delta$ is similar to $T$ or $T$ has a right angle, then $N$ is a
square or a sum of two squares \cite{BeesonI,SWW}. For a tile with an angle outside $\Q\pi$ the form in
which the classification is used here is a theorem of the library.

\begin{proposition}[Types of tiles; Laczkovich, in Beeson's organisation; \sF]\label{prop:types}
Let $\Delta$ be cut into $N$ copies of $T$, where some angle of $T$ is not a rational multiple of $\pi$ and
$\Delta$ is not similar to $T$. Then for some $\alpha\notin\Q\pi$ one of the following holds.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(R)}] $T$ has angles $(\alpha,\frac\pi2-\alpha,\frac\pi2)$, and two angles of $\Delta$ are equal
to $\alpha$, or two are equal to $\frac\pi2-\alpha$.
\item[\textup{(D)}] $T$ has angles $(\alpha,\pi-3\alpha,2\alpha)$, and two angles of $\Delta$ are equal to
$\alpha$.
\item[\textup{(S)}] $T$ has angles $(\alpha,\frac{2\pi}3-\alpha,\frac\pi3)$, and $\Delta$ is equilateral.
\item[\textup{(H)}] $T$ has angles $(\alpha,\beta,\frac{2\pi}3)$ with $\beta=\frac\pi3-\alpha$, and $\Delta$ is
equilateral, or has the angles $(\alpha,\alpha,\alpha+3\beta)$ or $(\beta,\beta,\beta+3\alpha)$, or the
angles of one of
\[
F_1=(\alpha,\alpha+\beta,\alpha+2\beta),\quad F_2=(2\alpha,2\beta,\alpha+\beta),\quad
F_3=(\alpha,2\alpha,3\beta),\quad F_4=(\alpha,2\beta,2\alpha+\beta),
\]
or of the triangles $F_1'$, $F_3'$, $F_4'$ obtained from these by exchanging $\alpha$ and $\beta$.
\item[\textup{(T)}] $T$ has angles $(\alpha,\beta,2\alpha+\beta)$ with $3\alpha+2\beta=\pi$, and $\Delta$ has
the angles $(\beta,\beta,3\alpha)$, $(\alpha+\beta,\alpha+\beta,\alpha)$, $(2\alpha,\beta,\alpha+\beta)$,
$(2\alpha,\alpha,2\beta)$ or $(\alpha,\alpha,\alpha+2\beta)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

The proof counts the angles of the tiles at the vertices of the tiling and at the corners of $\Delta$; with
an irrational angle the resulting equations between integers leave finitely many cases. The shapes of $\Delta$ in (H)
that are not equilateral are those of Zhang~\cite{Zhang}.

In case (H) we always write $(a,b,c)$ for the sides opposite $(\alpha,\beta,\frac{2\pi}3)$, so that
$c^2=a^2+ab+b^2$. A \emph{primitive triple} is a triple of positive integers with this relation and
$\gcd(a,b)=1$; then $a$, $b$, $c$ are pairwise coprime and $3\nmid c$.

\subsection{The tiles with $3\alpha+2\beta=\pi$}

A triangle with angles $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)$, $\gamma=2\alpha+\beta$ and $3\alpha+2\beta=\pi$, has
$\gamma=\frac{\pi+\alpha}2$ and $\beta=\frac{\pi-3\alpha}2$, so by the law of sines
\[
\frac ac=2\sin\frac\alpha2=:s,\qquad \frac bc=1-s^2 .
\]
If $s=e/f$ in lowest terms, the tile is similar to $(ef,\,f^2-e^2,\,f^2)$ with $1\le e<f$. Conversely, for
such $e,f$ the angle $\alpha$ of this triangle has $\sin\frac\alpha2=\frac e{2f}\in(0,\frac12)$ rational,
and $\alpha/\pi$ is irrational by Niven's theorem.

\begin{definition}\label{def:TW}
Let $1\le e<f$ be coprime integers, $(a,b,c)=(ef,\,f^2-e^2,\,f^2)$, and $M\ge1$ an integer. The triangle
$T_M(e,f)$ has sides $Mf^3$, $Mf^3$, $Me(3f^2-e^2)$ and angles $\beta,\beta,3\alpha$. The triangle
$W_M(e,f)$ has sides $Mf(f^2-e^2)$, $Me(2f^2-e^2)$, $Mf^3$ and angles $\beta$, $2\alpha$, $\alpha+\beta$
opposite these sides. A \emph{tiling} of $T_M(e,f)$ or of $W_M(e,f)$ is a tiling by copies of $(a,b,c)$; it
has $M^2(3f^2-e^2)$, respectively $M^2(2f^2-e^2)$, tiles. For $M\ge1$ we write $\tau(e,f,M)$ for the
statement: \emph{$T_M(e,f)$ has a tiling if and only if $M\ge f$.}
\end{definition}

The side $Mf(f^2-e^2)=fMb$ of $W_M(e,f)$ is the side $b$ of a copy of the tile at scale $fM$. Attached
along it, this copy completes $W_M(e,f)$ to $T_M(e,f)$, and the numbers of tiles agree:
$M^2(2f^2-e^2)+f^2M^2=M^2(3f^2-e^2)$. So a tiling of $W_M(e,f)$ gives a tiling of $T_M(e,f)$. A tiling of
$T_M(e,f)$ need not arise in this way, because the common side need not be a union of edges; so the absence of a tiling of $W_M(e,f)$ does not exclude a tiling of
$T_M(e,f)$.

\begin{lemma}[Vertex figures; \sF]\label{lem:figures}
Let $T$ have angles $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)$ with $3\alpha+2\beta=\pi$, $\gamma=2\alpha+\beta$,
$\alpha\notin\Q\pi$. If tiles showing $p$ angles $\alpha$, $q$ angles $\beta$ and $r$ angles $\gamma$ fill an
angle $\pi$ at a point, then $(p,q,r)=(3,2,0)$ or $(1,1,1)$; in particular at most one of them shows
$\gamma$. At a corner of angle $\beta$ there is one tile, showing $\beta$; at a corner of angle $3\alpha$
there are three tiles, each showing $\alpha$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
With $\beta=\frac{\pi-3\alpha}2$ and $\gamma=\frac{\pi+\alpha}2$,
$p\alpha+q\beta+r\gamma=\frac12\bigl((2p-3q+r)\alpha+(q+r)\pi\bigr)$. Setting this equal to $\pi$, $\beta$
or $3\alpha$ and using that $\alpha/\pi$ is irrational gives two linear equations in each case, whose
nonnegative solutions are the ones listed.
\end{proof}

The same computation at a corner of angle $\alpha+\beta$ gives two tiles, showing $\alpha$ and $\beta$.

\section{The signed-direction functional}\label{sec:invariant}

In this section $T$ has angles $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=(\alpha,\frac\pi3-\alpha,\frac{2\pi}3)$ with
$\alpha\notin\Q\pi$ and sides $(a,b,c)$, and $\Delta$ is cut into $N$ copies of $T$.

\subsection{Definition and cancellation}
Fix a reference direction along an edge of one tile. The angles of $T$ lie in $\Z\alpha+\Z\frac\pi3$, and
two tiles that meet at a point have edge directions there which differ by sums of angles of tiles and of
$\pi$; since $\Delta$ is connected, every edge of every tile has a direction $j\frac\pi3+k\alpha$ with
integers $j,k$. As $\frac\pi3$ and $\alpha$ are linearly independent over $\Q$, the direction determines
$k$, and $j$ modulo $6$. Put
\[
f_\alpha(\theta)=(-1)^j\quad\text{for }\theta=j\tfrac\pi3+k\alpha,\qquad
f_\beta(\theta)=(-1)^{j'}\quad\text{for }\theta=j'\tfrac\pi3+k'\beta .
\]
Both are defined on all directions that occur, $f_\beta=(-1)^kf_\alpha$, and
$f(\theta+\pi)=-f(\theta)$ for $f\in\{f_\alpha,f_\beta\}$, since $\pi=3\cdot\frac\pi3$. The \emph{weight}
of a directed segment of length $L$ and direction $\theta$ is $Lf(\theta)$, and $\Phi_f(\partial P)$ denotes
the sum of the weights of the three sides of a triangle $P$, directed counterclockwise.

\begin{lemma}[Cancellation; \sF]\label{lem:cancel}
For $f\in\{f_\alpha,f_\beta\}$: $\ \sum_{i=1}^N\Phi_f(\partial t_i)=\Phi_f(\partial\Delta)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Cut every edge of every tile at every point where another edge meets it. The pieces obtained do not overlap
partially, and since the weight is linear in the length, the sums do not change. A piece in the interior of
$\Delta$ is covered once from each of its two sides, by tiles whose boundaries run along it in opposite
directions, so its two weights cancel. A piece on the boundary of $\Delta$ is covered by one tile, in the
counterclockwise direction of $\partial\Delta$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Value on a tile; \sF]\label{lem:value}
For every tile $t$ of the tiling, $\Phi_{f_\alpha}(\partial t)=\pm(c+a-b)$ and
$\Phi_{f_\beta}(\partial t)=\pm(c+b-a)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $V_1,V_2,V_3$ be the vertices of a positively oriented tile, counterclockwise, with angles
$\alpha,\beta,\gamma$. The directed edges $V_1V_2$, $V_2V_3$, $V_3V_1$ have lengths $c,a,b$, and the
direction turns by $\pi-\beta$ and then by $\pi-\gamma$. In the coordinates $j\frac\pi3+k\alpha$ the angle
$\pi$ adds $3$ to $j$, $\beta$ adds $1$ and $\gamma$ adds $2$; so $j$ takes the values $j_0,j_0+2,j_0+3$ and
the sum is $(-1)^{j_0}(c+a-b)$. In the coordinates $j'\frac\pi3+k'\beta$ the angle $\beta$ adds $0$ and
$\gamma$ adds $2$; so $j'$ takes the values $j_0',j_0'+3,j_0'+4$ and the sum is $(-1)^{j_0'}(c-a+b)$. A
reflected tile is traversed in the opposite sense, which changes the sign.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Integrality and parity; \sF]\label{cor:int}
The numbers
\[
M_\alpha=\frac{\Phi_{f_\alpha}(\partial\Delta)}{c+a-b},\qquad
M_\beta=\frac{\Phi_{f_\beta}(\partial\Delta)}{c+b-a}
\]
are integers, and $M_\alpha\equiv M_\beta\equiv N\pmod2$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By Lemmas~\ref{lem:cancel} and~\ref{lem:value} each of them is a sum of $N$ signs.
\end{proof}

A change of the reference direction changes $M_\alpha$ and $M_\beta$ at most by a sign. The two conditions
are separate necessary conditions for a tiling.

\subsection{The isosceles obstruction}

\begin{lemma}[Non-integrality; \sF]\label{lem:nonint}
Let $a,k$ be coprime positive integers, $b=k^2$, and let $c>0$ satisfy $c^2=a^2+ab+b^2$. Then $k$ does not
divide $a+b-c$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
We have $a<c<a+b$. If $k\mid a+b-c$, then $c\equiv a\pmod k$ and $c=a+kt$ with $1\le t\le k-1$. Substituting
and dividing by $k$ gives $a(2t-k)=k(k-t)(k+t)$. So $k\mid a(2t-k)$, hence $k\mid2t$, and $2\le2t\le2k-2$
gives $2t=k$. Then the left side is $0$ and the right side is positive.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Rationality from the two integers; \sF]\label{prop:rational}
Let $a,b,c>0$ with $c^2=a^2+ab+b^2$ and let $P\ne0$ be real. If $P^2b(a+2b)$, $P(c-a-b)$ and $P(c+a+b)$ are
integers, then $a/b$ and $c/b$ are rational.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Write $N$, $M_1$, $M_2$ for the three integers. Then $M_1M_2=P^2(c^2-(a+b)^2)=-P^2ab$, so
$N+M_1M_2=2P^2b^2\ne0$ and $a/b=-2M_1M_2/(N+M_1M_2)$. Also $M_2-M_1=2P(a+b)\ne0$ and $M_1+M_2=2Pc$, so
$c/(a+b)=(M_1+M_2)/(M_2-M_1)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:iso}]
Let $\Delta$ be isosceles, not equilateral, and tiled by a prime number $N$ of copies of $T$. By
Proposition~\ref{prop:types} its base angles are $\alpha$ or $\beta$: the other shapes of (H), and a
triangle similar to $T$, are scalene or equilateral, because distinct integer combinations of $\alpha$ and
$\frac\pi3$ are distinct angles. Exchanging the names of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ if necessary, let the base
angles be $\alpha$. Since $2\cos\alpha=(a+2b)/c$, the sides of $\Delta$ are $\lambda c$, $\lambda c$,
$\lambda(a+2b)$ for a real $\lambda>0$, and by Proposition~\ref{prop:basics}(i)
\[
N=\frac{\lambda^2(a+2b)}b .
\]
Take the reference direction along the base. The legs have the directions $\pi-\alpha$ and $\pi+\alpha$,
with $j=3$ in the coordinates of $f_\alpha$ and $j'=2$, $j'=4$ in those of $f_\beta$. Hence
$\Phi_{f_\alpha}(\partial\Delta)=\lambda(a+2b-2c)$ and $\Phi_{f_\beta}(\partial\Delta)=\lambda(a+2b+2c)$, and
the identities $(c-a-b)(c+a-b)=b(a+2b-2c)$ and $(c+a+b)(c+b-a)=b(a+2b+2c)$ give
\[
M_\alpha=\frac{\lambda(c-a-b)}b,\qquad M_\beta=\frac{\lambda(c+a+b)}b .
\]
By Corollary~\ref{cor:int} these are integers, and Proposition~\ref{prop:rational} with $P=\lambda/b$ shows
that the sides of $T$ are commensurable. Scale the figure so that $(a,b,c)$ is a primitive triple. The sides
$\lambda c$ and $\lambda(a+2b)$ of $\Delta$ are sums of edges, hence integers, so $\lambda$ is rational. A
common prime factor of $c$ and $a+2b$ would divide $(a+2b)^2-c^2=3b(a+b)$; it does not divide $b$ or $a+b$,
since then it would divide $a$ and $b$, and it is not $3$, since $3\nmid c$. So $c$ and $a+2b$ are coprime
and $\lambda$ is an integer. Now $Nb=\lambda^2(a+2b)$ with $\gcd(b,a+2b)=1$ gives
$b\mid\lambda^2$ and $N=(\lambda^2/b)(a+2b)$. As $N$ is prime and $a+2b\ge3$, we get $\lambda^2=b$. Then
$M_\alpha=(c-a-b)/\lambda$ is an integer, with $b=\lambda^2$ and $\gcd(a,\lambda)=1$, against
Lemma~\ref{lem:nonint}.
\end{proof}

Both functionals are used: together they give the rationality of the tile, and on the target with base
angles $\beta$ their roles are exchanged. The formal statement of Theorem~\ref{thm:iso} has no hypothesis of
rationality on the tile.

\subsection{The numbers of tiles on the other targets}

\begin{proposition}[Forms of $N$; \sF]\label{prop:forms}
Let $(a,b,c)$ be a primitive triple and let $\Delta$ be cut into $N$ copies of a triangle similar to
$(a,b,c)$, where $a,b,c$ are opposite $\alpha,\beta,\frac{2\pi}3$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(i)}] If $\Delta$ has the angles of $F_2$, $F_3$ or $F_4$, then $N=N_0k^2$ for an integer
$k\ge1$, with $N_0=(a+2b)(2a+b)$, $3(a+b)(a+2b)$, $(a+b)(2a+b)$ respectively.
\item[\textup{(ii)}] If $\Delta$ has the angles of $F_1$ and $b=de^2$ with $d$ squarefree, then
$N=dw^2(a+b)$ for an integer $w\ge1$.
\item[\textup{(iii)}] If $\Delta$ is similar to the tile, $N$ is a square.
\item[\textup{(iv)}] If $\Delta$ is equilateral with side $S$, in the unit in which the tile is $(a,b,c)$,
then $S$ is an integer, $N=S^2/(ab)$, and $c+a-b$ and $c+b-a$ divide $3S$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[The isosceles and $F_1$ targets; arithmetic; \sF]\label{prop:spectrum}
Let $(a,b,c)$ be a primitive triple and $N\ge1$. There are integers $k\ge1$ and
$M_\alpha\equiv M_\beta\equiv N\pmod2$ with
\[
Nb=k^2(a+2b),\qquad M_\alpha(c+a-b)=k(a+2b-2c),\qquad M_\beta(c+b-a)=k(a+2b+2c)
\]
if and only if $N=m^2b(a+2b)$ for an integer $m\ge1$. The corresponding statement holds with the equations
of the isosceles target with base angles $\beta$ and $N=m^2a(2a+b)$, and with those of $F_1$ and
$N=m^2b(a+b)$.
\end{proposition}

The three equations are the area and the two integers of Corollary~\ref{cor:int} on the isosceles target
with base angles $\alpha$ at scale $k$. So the numbers of tiles that these conditions allow on the isosceles
targets and on $F_1$ are exactly the numbers of the families constructed by Zhang~\cite{Zhang}, who
realizes them for $m$ in certain residue classes from a bound on. The conditions are necessary and not
sufficient: $33=3\cdot11$ has this form for the tile $(5,3,7)$ and is not a tile count
(Theorem~\ref{thm:200}). Which small members are realized is part of what is open
(Section~\ref{sec:open}).

\section{The prime case}\label{sec:prime}

\subsection{The primes that arithmetic does not exclude}

\begin{proposition}[\sF]\label{prop:mod12}
Let $p>3$ be a prime. There are coprime integers $1\le e<f$ with $p=3f^2-e^2$ if and only if
$p\equiv11\pmod{12}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $p=3f^2-e^2$. If $3\mid e$ then $3\mid p$; so $p\equiv-e^2\equiv2\pmod3$. The integers $e,f$ are not both
even, and not both odd since then $p\equiv2\pmod4$; in the two remaining cases $p\equiv3\pmod4$. Conversely
let $p\equiv11\pmod{12}$. By quadratic reciprocity $3$ is a square modulo $p$, so $p$ is represented by a
binary quadratic form of discriminant $12$. There are two classes of such forms, represented by $x^2-3y^2$
and $3y^2-x^2$, and the first represents only primes $\equiv1\pmod3$. So $p=3y^2-x^2$ with positive integers
$x,y$, coprime because $p$ is prime. If $x\ge y$ then $y<x<\sqrt3\,y$, and $(|2x-3y|,\,2y-x)$ is another
representation with a smaller second entry; so the representation with $y$ least has $x<y$.
\end{proof}

A prime has at most one such representation (\lean{ThinHole.rep_unique}).

\subsection{The case analysis}\label{sub:cases}
Let $N$ be a prime with $N\equiv3\pmod4$ and $N>3$, and suppose that $\Delta$ is cut into $N$ copies of
$T$. The formal proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:primes} excludes this in five steps.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[1.] \emph{All angles of $T$ are rational multiples of $\pi$.} Write the angles as $a_k\pi/n$ with

\item[3.] \emph{Otherwise} $T$ and $\Delta$ are of one of the types of Proposition~\ref{prop:types}.

\subsection{The base-$\beta$ targets at scale one}\label{sub:scaleone}
Throughout, $(a,b,c)=(ef,f^2-e^2,f^2)$ with coprime $1\le e<f$. The proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:scaleone} has
this architecture.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[1.] \emph{Normal form.} A dissection whose target and tile have the stated sides is carried by an
isometry to a fixed position; the vertex figures are those of Lemma~\ref{lem:figures}; the sides are words
in $a,b,c$ (\lean{BaseBetaWalks.lean}, \lean{E1NormalForm.normal_form}).
\item[2.] \emph{The members $(1,f)$, $f\ge3$.} The base carries exactly one $b$-edge, with $f$ $a$-edges and
one $c$-edge, or two $c$-edges, or $2f$ $a$-edges. Each word is excluded by the figures at its junctions and
the partition lemma applied to segments above the base
(\lean{E1BaseWord.e1_family_f_ge_3_of_sides}).
\item[3.] \emph{The pierced corner.} Lemma~\ref{lem:pierce} below, which holds for every member.
\item[4.] \emph{The members $(f-1,f)$, $f\ge2$.} Here $a+c=fb$, and only here. This places the corner $V$ of
Lemma~\ref{lem:pierce} one length $a$ above the base. A tile showing $\alpha$ or $\beta$ at $V$ against the
continuation of the ray would have a vertex below the base. In the remaining arrangement, $\gamma$ against
the ray and $\alpha$ against the edge of $t_2$, a segment of length $a-b$ is left, and $a-b$ is not a sum of
edges (\lean{R1Final.r1_no_tiling}).
\item[5.] \emph{The members with $2\le e\le f-2$.} The pierced corner forces a patch of translates of the
apex tiles along the ray of Lemma~\ref{lem:pierce}. At the boundary of the patch the remaining angle allows
two continuations, and every continuation that leaves the lattice is excluded by an induction along a row,
by a reflection lemma, or by a march along two converging lines of the lattice; an induction along the
other inner ray ends the argument
(\lean{TheoremHCongruent.theorem_H_of_sides}).
\end{enumerate}
The three families of steps 2, 4 and 5 cover all members; $(1,2)$ falls under step 4. The assembled
statement is \lean{BaseBetaFullUnc.basebeta_full_unc}. For steps 4 and 5 the proof is the formal one: the
statements of the intermediate lemmas are written out, but there is no continuous written proof. This is part
of the standing recorded after Theorem~\ref{thm:primes}.

\begin{lemma}[The pierced corner; \sF]\label{lem:pierce}
Let a triangle with angles $(\beta,\beta,3\alpha)$ be tiled by copies of $(a,b,c)$, and suppose that the
edges of the two legs at the apex are $c$-edges. Let $t_2$ be the middle one of the three tiles at the apex.
Then one of the two edges of $t_2$ at the apex is its $c$-edge and lies against the $b$-edge of a
neighbouring apex tile, and the vertex $V$ of $t_2$ at the far end of this $c$-edge lies in the relative
interior of an edge of another tile. The tiles that have a vertex at $V$ on the side of $t_2$, other than
$t_2$, show the angles $\{\alpha,\gamma\}$ or $\{\alpha,\alpha,\alpha,\beta\}$.
\end{lemma}

At scale one the hypothesis holds, since a leg of $T_1(e,f)$ carries no $b$-edge: every side of the target
carries a $c$-edge (\lean{Dissection.congruentDissection_gammatrap}), and a sum of edges of length $f^3$ that
contains a $c$ contains no $b$ (\lean{BaseBetaWalks.side_no_b}).

\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:figures} three tiles show $\alpha$ at the apex, and the edges of a tile at its vertex of
angle $\alpha$ are $b$ and $c$. The outer two lay $c$ on the legs, so they turn their $b$-edges inward, and
$t_2$ turns one $b$-edge and one $c$-edge inward. On the ray where the $c$-edge of $t_2$ meets a $b$-edge,
the $b$-edge ends at distance $b$ from the apex, in the relative interior of the $c$-edge. Beyond that point
the $c$-edge of $t_2$ has the length $c-b=e^2$ left, and the tiles on its other side lay consecutive edges
along it, starting at that point. But $e^2$ is not a sum of edges: $ef>e^2$ and $f^2>e^2$, and
$n(f^2-e^2)=e^2$ would give $f^2\mid n+1$, hence $e^2\ge f^2-1$ and $f^2-e^2=1$, which is impossible. So one
of these edges passes through the end $V$ of the $c$-edge. The tiles at $V$ on the side of $t_2$ fill the angle $\pi$, of which $t_2$ takes $\beta$, and by
Lemma~\ref{lem:figures} the others show $\{\alpha,\gamma\}$ or $\{\alpha,\alpha,\alpha,\beta\}$.
\end{proof}

\section{Equilateral targets: commensurable sides}\label{sec:pairs}

Let an equilateral triangle of side $S$ be cut into $N$ copies of a tile of type (S) or (H), with sides
$(a,b,c)$ opposite $(\alpha,\beta,\frac\pi3)$, respectively $(\alpha,\beta,\frac{2\pi}3)$, and
$\alpha\notin\Q\pi$.

\begin{proposition}[The pair of a tiling; \sF]\label{prop:pairs}
For a tile of type \textup{(S)} there are integers $m_1>m_2\ge1$, with $m_1\le N$ and
$m_1\equiv m_2\equiv N\pmod2$, such that
\[
m_1(a+b-c)=3S=m_2(a+b+c),\qquad S^2=Nab,\qquad m_1m_2=3N .
\]
For a tile of type \textup{(H)} there are integers $1\le s,t\le N$ with $s\equiv t\equiv N\pmod2$ such that
\[
s(c+a-b)=3S=t(c+b-a),\qquad S^2=Nab,\qquad st=3N .
\]
\end{proposition}

For type (H) the integers are $M_\alpha$ and $M_\beta$ of Corollary~\ref{cor:int}: the three sides of the
target differ in direction by $\frac{2\pi}3$, so they have the same weight. Type (S) is analogous. From the
displayed equations,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:disc}
(m_1+m_2)^2-16N=\Bigl(\frac{2N(a-b)}S\Bigr)^2,\qquad (t-s)^2+16N=\Bigl(\frac{2N(a+b)}S\Bigr)^2 .
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Laczkovich; Beeson and Zhang; \sF]\label{thm:relations}
In the situation above, for a tile of either type, there are integers $j\ge1$ and $p,q\ge0$ with
$ja=pb+qc$, and integers $j'\ge1$, $p',q'\ge0$ with $j'b=p'a+q'c$. Consequently the sides $a,b,c$ are
pairwise commensurable.
\end{theorem}

For type (S) the commensurability is a theorem of Laczkovich \cite[Thm.~3.3]{Laczkovich2012}. For type (H)
the relations and the commensurability are Lemma~3.5 and Theorem~5.2 of Beeson and Zhang~\cite{BeesonZhang}.
The proof formalized is their degree count, for type (S) with the roles of two vertex figures exchanged, and
with a different last step for type (H). What is added is the formal proof.

\begin{proof}[Architecture, for type \textup{(S)}]
A \emph{vertex} is a vertex of some tile, and
its figure $(x,y,z)$ counts the tiles showing $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ there.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[1.] Since $\alpha\notin\Q\pi$, every figure has $x=y$; it is $(0,0,1)$ at a corner of the target,
$(1,1,1)$ or $(0,0,3)$ at a vertex where the angles add up to $\pi$, and $(3,3,0)$, $(2,2,2)$, $(1,1,4)$ or
$(0,0,6)$ at a vertex where they add up to $2\pi$.
\item[2.] Every tile has one angle of each kind, so $\sum_V(z_V-x_V)=0$. With $\mathcal O$ the set of
vertices of figure $(3,3,0)$ or $(1,1,4)$ and $\mathcal T$ the set of interior vertices of figure $(0,0,3)$,
this gives $|\mathcal O|\ge|\mathcal T|+1$.
\item[3.] Suppose no relation $ja=pb+qc$ holds. Call a directed $a$-edge from a vertex \emph{mismatched} if
another tile has an edge of length $b$ or $c$ from the same vertex along the same ray. On its side of the
line the $a$-edges continue until a first point $H$, its \emph{head}, where the next edge is not an
$a$-edge; by the hypothesis no vertex of the other side lies on the way, so $H$ is an interior vertex where
the angles add up to $\pi$.
\item[4.] Different mismatched edges have different heads; a mismatched edge starts at every vertex of
$\mathcal O$, where the number of $a$-edges is odd, and at every head of figure $(1,1,1)$.
\item[5.] Counting mismatched edges by their heads and by their starting points gives
$|\mathcal O|\le|\mathcal T|$, against step 2.
\end{enumerate}
The second relation follows by exchanging $\alpha$ and $\beta$, and the two relations with
Proposition~\ref{prop:pairs} give the commensurability.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Pairs with a non-square discriminant; \sF]\label{cor:nonsquare}
In Proposition~\ref{prop:pairs} the integer $(m_1+m_2)^2-16N$, respectively $(t-s)^2+16N$, is a perfect
square. So for every $N$, a pair $(m_1,m_2)$ or $(s,t)$ for which this integer is not a square is the pair of
no tiling.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:relations} the sides $a,b,c$ are commensurable, and $S$ is commensurable with them by
the first equation of Proposition~\ref{prop:pairs}. So the right-hand sides of \eqref{eq:disc} are squares
of rational numbers, and the left-hand sides are integers.
\end{proof}

For a given $N$ there are finitely many pairs, and each pair determines the tile up to scale, with $S$. The
corollary removes the pairs whose tile would have incommensurable sides without any search. For type (S)
the formal proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:relations} does not use Theorem~\ref{thm:scaleone}. The hypotheses of
the theorem are satisfied by kernel-checked tilings with $1440$ tiles, for type (S), and $1215$ tiles, for
type (H), in the library.

\subsection{The value $135$}

\begin{proposition}[\sF]\label{prop:n135}
$135$ is a tile count if and only if some equilateral triangle has a tiling by $135$ copies of a tile of
type \textup{(H)} whose pair $(s,t)$ of Proposition~\ref{prop:pairs} is $(15,27)$ or $(27,15)$.
\end{proposition}

A tiling with this pair has its tile similar to $(3,5,7)$ and the side $45$ in the unit of the tile; this
identification is elementary and is not part of the formal statement. So the question is whether the
equilateral triangle of side $45$ can be cut into $135$ copies of $(3,5,7)$. Every other case for $135$ is
closed in Lean: the tiles with angles in $\Q\pi$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:gapA}, the pair $(9,45)$ by
Corollary~\ref{cor:nonsquare}, and two pairs of the types (D) and (T) by certificates.

\begin{theorem}[\sF]\label{thm:n135}
The equilateral triangle of side $45$ cannot be cut into $135$ copies of the triangle $(3,5,7)$. Consequently
$135$ is not a tile count.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof by certificate]
A search places the tiles at the lowest uncovered point, one corner at a time, and records why every branch
fails. Its tree has $1\,326\,256$ nodes in exact arithmetic over $\Q(\sqrt3)$, with $81\,629$ learned sets of tiles
that no tiling contains. Only the half of the tree whose first tile shows the angle $\alpha$ on the base is written
out; the other half is refuted through the mirror image of the target. The tree is cut into $2766$ files, and the
Lean kernel checks each node against the geometry of the library. The second sentence follows with Proposition~\ref{prop:n135}.
\end{proof}

Paliy \cite{PaliyLedger} has announced that $15m^2$ is a number of tiles for this tile exactly when
$m\ge4$, with a computer-assisted exclusion of $m=3$. Theorem~\ref{thm:n135} reproduces that exclusion with a
certificate of our own that the Lean kernel has checked; the claim is his. For $m=4$ the tiling of the
equilateral triangle of side $60$ by $240$ copies, which is also his, is kernel-checked here
(\lean{N240.equilateral_60_by_357}). Gluing two tiled equilateral triangles of sides $3k$ and $5l$ at two corners of the triangle of side $3k+5l$ and filling the rest with a $k\times l$ grid of parallelograms with sides $3$ and $5$, each cut into two tiles along its diagonal of length $7$, tiles the larger side; with the seeds $m=4$ and $m=9$ (Herdt's tiling, \cite{Zhang}) this gives a tiling of the equilateral triangle of side $15m$ for every $m$ in the additive semigroup generated by $4$ and $9$ (\lean{EqSemigroup.eq357_semigroup}); adding two copies of the tile scaled by $3m$ at two corners (Zhang's Proposition~8(2) \cite{Zhang}) gives the isosceles triangle $(21m,21m,33m)$ with $33m^2$ tiles for the same $m$ (\lean{Iso357Semigroup.iso357_semigroup}), including $m=4,8$, below the range of Zhang's construction; for $m=1,2$ the numbers $15$ and $60$ are not tile counts
(Theorem~\ref{thm:200}).

\subsection{Statements for infinitely many $N$}\label{sub:infinite}

\begin{proposition}[Isosceles targets with base angles $\alpha+\beta$; \sF]\label{prop:atoms}
Let $0<e<f$ be coprime, $(a,b,c)=(ef,f^2-e^2,f^2)$, and $t\ge1$ an integer.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(i)}] If the triangle with sides $tfb$, $tfb$, $teb$ is cut into copies of $(a,b,c)$, then
$teb=Pa+Qb+Rc$ with integers $P,Q\ge0$ and $R\ge2$.
\item[\textup{(ii)}] For every $k\ge1$ the triangle with sides $2(k+1)(2k+1)$, $2(k+1)(2k+1)$, $2k(2k+1)$
cannot be cut into copies of $(k(k+1),\,2k+1,\,(k+1)^2)$.
\item[\textup{(iii)}] Let $3\alpha+2\beta=\pi$ with $\alpha\notin\Q\pi$. If an isosceles triangle with base
angles $\alpha+\beta$ is cut into $N$ congruent triangles with angles $(\alpha,\beta,2\alpha+\beta)$, then
$N=t^2(f^2-e^2)$ for integers $t\ge2$ and coprime $0<e<f\le te$. In particular $N$ is neither prime nor
squarefree.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

Part (i) is the conclusion of \cite[Lem.~6]{BeesonIII} for this side. As usually quoted, that every side of
the target carries two $c$-edges, the lemma fails for the dissection of $(4,6,8)$ into four copies of
$(2,3,4)$; what is added is a proof under explicit hypotheses. Part (ii) is the case $(e,f,t)=(k,k+1,2)$,
where the base has no representation with $R\ge2$, and is used for $N=124$, $140$, $172$ and $188$. Part
(iii) contains the prime case of this target, and its proof does not use Theorem~\ref{thm:scaleone}.

\begin{proposition}[Angles in $\Q\pi$ at $15s^2$ and $30s^2$; \sF]\label{prop:gapA}
Let $N=ds^2$ with $d\in\{15,30\}$ and $s\ge1$. No triangle can be cut into $N$ congruent triangles all of
whose angles are rational multiples of $\pi$.
\end{proposition}

For these $N$ the statement is contained in the result of \cite{BeesonSeven} quoted in
Section~\ref{sub:types}; the proposition is a formal proof of this case. It uses an identity between sines,
which is divided by $s^2$ and refuted, for the ten tiles that a rank criterion does not exclude, by
certificates in cyclotomic fields for $9\,280$ pairs of a tile and a target.

\begin{proposition}[Twice a prime; \sF]\label{prop:twoprime}
Let $p$ be a prime. Then $2p$ is a tile count if and only if $p=3$ or $p\not\equiv3\pmod4$.
\end{proposition}

For $p\equiv3\pmod8$ the exclusion follows from Theorem~A of Paliy \cite{PaliySectors}. The formal proof
uses Theorem~\ref{thm:scaleone}.

\begin{theorem}[Squarefree $N$ prime to $3$, by residue modulo $8$; \sF]\label{thm:sqfree}
Let $N$ be squarefree with $3\nmid N$, and suppose that $N$ is a tile count.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(i)}] $N\not\equiv3\pmod8$.
\item[\textup{(ii)}] If $N\equiv7\pmod8$, then $N=ab$ or $N=(a+2b)(2a+b)$ for positive integers $a,b$ with
$a^2+ab+b^2$ a square. Below $455$ the only such tile count is $143$.
\item[\textup{(iii)}] If $N\equiv5\pmod8$, then $N$ is a sum of two squares, or
$N=(2q^2-p^2)(3q^2-p^2)$ for coprime $0<p<q$, or $N=(f^2-e^2)(2f^2-e^2)$ for coprime $1\le e<f$. Below
$517$ the only such tile count that is not a sum of two squares is $77$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

Most of (i) reproduces a result of Paliy: the reduction of every tiling with such an $N$ to a tiling of some
$T_1(e,f)$ with $N=3f^2-e^2$ is the general clause of Theorem~A of \cite{PaliyReduction}, which excludes
every $N$ of the class for which this equation has no solution. Added here are a formal proof of the
reduction and the case in which the equation has a solution, by Theorem~\ref{thm:scaleone}. All three parts
use Theorem~\ref{thm:scaleone} and inherit its standing.

\begin{remark}[Squarefree $N$ in general]\label{rem:c3}
There is a decidable arithmetic condition on a squarefree $N$, with one clause for each case of the
classification, which implies that $N$ is not a tile count; the implication is a Lean theorem
(\lean{AllN3.not_tile_of_conditions3}).
\end{remark}

\begin{computation}[\sS]\label{comp:c3}
Of the $60\,789$ squarefree $N$ with $7\le N\le10^5$, $13\,600$ are sums of two squares, $46\,898$ satisfy
the condition of Remark~\ref{rem:c3}, and $291$ are neither. Those of the $291$ that are below $1000$ are
$7$, $11$, $15$, $21$, $30$, $33$, $70$, $77$, $105$, $143$, $154$, $161$, $231$, $322$, $329$, $330$,
$345$, $354$, $385$, $455$, $465$, $506$, $517$, $546$, $777$, $806$, $897$, $935$, $966$.
\end{computation}

The count is by a program that transcribes the condition and is not checked by the kernel value by value.
Of the values listed, those up to $161$ are decided by Theorems~\ref{thm:primes} and~\ref{thm:200}.

\section{The base-$\beta$ targets above scale one}\label{sec:row}

\subsection{Existence}

\begin{theorem}[The seed at scale $f$; Beeson, Paliy; \sF]\label{thm:seed}
Let $1\le e<f$ be integers. The triangle with sides $f^2(f^2-e^2)$, $ef(2f^2-e^2)$, $f^4$ can be cut into
$f^2(2f^2-e^2)$ copies, and the triangle with sides $f^4$, $f^4$, $ef(3f^2-e^2)$ into $f^2(3f^2-e^2)$ copies,
of $(ef,\,f^2-e^2,\,f^2)$. For coprime $e,f$ these triangles are $W_f(e,f)$ and $T_f(e,f)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write $a=ef$, $b=f^2-e^2$, $c=f^2$, $P=3f^2-e^2$. Let $g_1,g_2$ be unit vectors with
$g_1\cdot g_2=-\cos\beta=-eP/(2f^3)$, and use the coordinates $(x,y)\mapsto xg_1+yg_2$. The parallelogram
$[0,a]\times[0,c]$ has a diagonal of length $b$, since $a^2+c^2-2ac\cos\beta=b^2$; it is two copies of the
tile. Put
\begin{gather*}
O=(0,0),\quad D=(ab,0),\quad E=(0,-a^2),\quad B=(ab,-a^2),\\
A=(ab,bc),\quad C=(-ac,-a^2),\quad Z=(-2ac,-a^2).
\end{gather*}
A computation with the quadratic form $x^2+y^2-xyeP/f^3$ gives $|AZ|=|AB|=c^2$ and $|ZB|=aP$, so $AZB$ is
the second triangle of the statement. The triangles $OCA$ and $ODA$ are similar to the tile with ratio $b$,
the triangle $OEC$ with ratio $a$, and the triangle $CZA$ with ratio $c$; each is cut into tiles by the
lines parallel to its sides. The parallelogram $ODBE$ is $[0,ab]\times[-a^2,0]$, which is $b\times e^2$
cells $[0,a]\times[0,c]$. These five pieces lie in $AZB$ and are separated by the lines $OA$, $OC$, $CA$,
$x=0$ and $y=0$. Their numbers of tiles add up to
\[
2b^2+a^2+2be^2+c^2=f^2(3f^2-e^2),
\]
the number that the area of $AZB$ requires, so they cover it. The first four pieces form the triangle $ACB$,
with sides $|AC|=bc$, $|CB|=a(b+c)$, $|BA|=c^2$ and $2b^2+a^2+2be^2=f^2(2f^2-e^2)$ tiles.
\end{proof}

The four pieces of $ACB$ are the dissection that Paliy's notes \cite{PaliyLedger} credit to Beeson
\cite[Thm.~11]{BeesonIII}, and the fifth piece is the ``beta lift'' of the same notes. The proof above, with
all seven points at integer coordinates in one frame, is the form in which it is formalized.

\begin{proof}[Architecture of the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:existence}]
Put $Q=2f^2-e^2$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[1.] \emph{Seed.} $W_f(e,f)$ has a tiling (Theorem~\ref{thm:seed}).
\item[2.] \emph{Collar.} For $T>f-e$, a tiling of $W_T$ extends to a tiling of $W_{T+e}$: the triangle
$W_{T+e}$ is the union of a translate of $W_T$, two rectangles of a skew frame tiled by parallelograms
$a\times b$, four triangles similar to the tile, and two parallelograms tiled along other lattices of the
tile. The nine pieces are separated by lines and contain $Q(T+e)^2$ tiles in all
(\lean{CollarG.collar_nat}).
\item[3.] \emph{Further seeds.} For $1\le t<e$ the triangle $W_{f+t}$ is the union of two truncated scaled
tiles, one scaled tile, and a hexagon cut into $e+t$ strips of triangles and parallelograms and one more
scaled tile; the numbers of tiles add up to $Q(f+t)^2$
(\lean{Residue.wTilable_residue}).
\item[4.] \emph{All scales.} Every $M\ge f$ is $f+t+ie$ with $0\le t<e$ and $i\ge0$, and $f+t>f-e$; so
steps 1 to 3 give $W_M$ (\lean{Residue.wTilable_ge}).
\item[5.] \emph{Lift.} $T_M(e,f)$ is $W_M(e,f)$ with a tile at scale $fM$ attached
(Definition~\ref{def:TW}; \lean{Residue.betaTilable_ge}).
\end{enumerate}
In the formal proof the parameters of the layouts are real numbers, every separating inequality is
certified by a polynomial with nonnegative coefficients in the parameters, and integrality is used only for
the numbers of rows and columns.
\end{proof}

The collar of step 2 and the seeds of step 3 are Paliy's \cite{PaliyLedger}. For $e=1$ step 3 is empty.

\subsection{Scale two for $f\le4$}

\begin{proposition}[\sF]\label{prop:scale2}
For $(e,f)\in\{(1,3),(2,3),(1,4),(3,4)\}$ the triangle $T_2(e,f)$ has no tiling by any number of copies of
$(ef,f^2-e^2,f^2)$. The triangle $T_2(1,2)=(16,16,22)$ is cut into $44$ copies of $(2,3,4)$.
\end{proposition}

The four exclusions are search certificates, of $56\,016$, $31\,709$, $56\,629$ and $234\,239$ nodes, for
the targets $(54,54,52)$, $(54,54,92)$, $(128,128,94)$ and $(128,128,234)$.

\subsection{The members $(1,f)$ below the scale $f$}\label{sub:below}

\begin{proof}[Architecture of the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:siderun}]
Place $s$ on a horizontal line with $\Delta$
above it. A tile with an edge on $s$ has one of six types,
named by the edge and the angles at its left and right ends: $A_1=(a;\beta,\gamma)$,
$A_2=(a;\gamma,\beta)$, $B_1=(b;\alpha,\gamma)$, $B_2=(b;\gamma,\alpha)$, $C_1=(c;\alpha,\beta)$,
$C_2=(c;\beta,\alpha)$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[1.] \emph{Tools.} The figures of Lemma~\ref{lem:figures}, the partition lemma, and three facts of
arithmetic: $jb$ is a sum of edges only as $b+\dots+b$ for $1\le j<f$; a representation of $jc$ with $j<f$
contains no $b$; and $ja<b$ for $j\le f-1$ (\lean{SideRun.rep_jb}, \lean{SideRun.rep_qc}).
\item[2.] \emph{Lattice.} Above consecutive tiles $X_0,\dots,X_{k-1}$ of type $C_1$ consider the lattice
with the vectors of the sides $c$ and $b$ of $X_0$; its triangles are copies of the tile, and $X_i$ is one
of them.
\item[3.] \emph{Run lemma.} For $k<f$ the following cannot hold together: the tile beside $X_0$ across its
$b$-edge shows $\beta$ at the left end of the run, and the right end of the run is a corner of angle $\beta$
of $\Delta$, or the next tile on $s$ shows $\gamma$ or $\beta$ there. The proof is an induction over the
rows of the lattice triangle above the run: each row is a segment clamped at both ends, the partition lemma
and the arithmetic of step 1 force its tiles, and the last chord of the triangle cannot be covered
(\lean{SideRun.Ctx.run_dies}).
\item[4.] \emph{Rules.} If all runs of $C_1$ and of $C_2$ on $s$ are shorter than $f$, then by step 3 and
its mirror image a maximal run that follows a tile of type $A_1$ or $B_1$, or starts at the left corner, is
followed by a tile of type $A_1$ or $B_1$, directly or after a second run (\lean{SideRun.Setup.side_rules}).
\item[5.] \emph{The word.} The first tile of $s$ is not $A_2$ or $B_2$, the last one is $A_2$ or $C_1$, and
$A_1$, $B_1$ are never followed by $A_2$, $B_2$. With step 4, after every tile of type $A_1$ or $B_1$ there
is a later one, which is impossible on a finite side (\lean{SideRun.Setup.side_theorem'}).
\end{enumerate}
Consecutive tiles of one type $C_1$ or $C_2$ are translates of one another, and $f$ of them cover a length
$fc=f^3$ of $s$.
\end{proof}

The hypotheses of Theorem~\ref{thm:siderun} are satisfied by the tiling of $W_f(1,f)$ of
Theorem~\ref{thm:seed} (\lean{SideRun.side_theorem_witness}).

\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:below}]
Let $(a,b,c)=(f,f^2-1,f^2)$. If a side of integer length $n$ carries $x$, $y$, $z$ edges of lengths $a$,
$b$, $c$, then $n=xf+y(f^2-1)+zf^2\equiv-y\pmod f$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:modf}
y\equiv-n\pmod f .
\end{equation}

(a) Let $M<f$ and suppose $W_M(1,f)$ has a tiling. The side of length $n=M(2f^2-1)$ lies opposite the angle
$2\alpha$, so its corners have the angles $\alpha+\beta$ and $\beta$. By \eqref{eq:modf}, $y\equiv M\pmod f$,
hence $y\ge M$, because $0<M<f$. Then $zf^2\le M(2f^2-1)-M(f^2-1)=Mf^2$ and $z\le M<f$. But by
Theorem~\ref{thm:siderun} this side carries at least $f$ tiles with their side $c$ on it. For $M\ge f$ a
tiling exists by Theorem~\ref{thm:existence}.

(b) Let $2M\le f$ and suppose $T_M(1,f)$ has a tiling. Both corners of the base have the angle $\beta$, and
its length is $n=M(3f^2-1)$. As before $y\ge M$, so $zf^2\le M(3f^2-1)-M(f^2-1)=2Mf^2$ and $z\le2M$, while
Theorem~\ref{thm:siderun} gives $z\ge f$. So $2M=f$ and $z=f$. Then
$xf+y(f^2-1)=M(3f^2-1)-f^3=M(f^2-1)$ with $y\ge M$ gives $y=M$ and $x=0$: the base carries no $a$-edge, and
its $f$ tiles with $c$ on the base are the tiles of Theorem~\ref{thm:siderun}, all of one type. But the
tile at each base corner shows $\beta$ there and its edges at the corner are $a$ and $c$
(Lemma~\ref{lem:figures}); without $a$-edges the first tile of the base is of type $C_2$ and the last one of
type $C_1$. This is a contradiction.
\end{proof}

At $M=1$ part (b) gives Theorem~\ref{thm:scaleone} for the members $(1,f)$, $f\ge2$, by a second route. For $T_2(1,f)$ part (b) covers
$f\ge4$, and $f=3$ is Proposition~\ref{prop:scale2}; so $\tau(1,f,2)$ holds for every $f\ge2$
(\lean{E1ScaleTwo.thresholdAt_1_f_2}). For $f/2<M<f$ the count allows a run of $f$ tiles on the base and
the argument gives nothing. For $e\ge2$ the formal proof does not apply: the first two arithmetic facts of
step 1 hold for every member, but $(f-1)a<b$ fails when $e\ge2$. Section~\ref{sub:allmembers} describes a
proof that does not use this fact.

\subsection{Every member below the scale $f$}\label{sub:allmembers}
Theorem~\ref{thm:siderunall}, Corollaries~\ref{cor:ebelow} and~\ref{cor:thickw} and
Proposition~\ref{prop:cornerrun} are theorems of the Lean library (Table~\ref{tab:map}); below we give the
architecture of the proofs and the short arguments in full. The hypotheses of the formal version of
Theorem~\ref{thm:siderunall} are shown to hold for the tilings of Theorem~\ref{thm:existence}, for every
member. For $e=1$, Theorem~\ref{thm:siderunall} is
Theorem~\ref{thm:siderun} and Corollary~\ref{cor:ebelow} is contained in Corollary~\ref{cor:below}, which
are formal.

\begin{proof}[Changes to the architecture for Theorem~\ref{thm:siderunall}]
The five steps are those of the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:siderun} in Section~\ref{sub:below}, with the
tile $(a,b,c)=(ef,f^2-e^2,f^2)$ and the following changes.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[1.] \emph{Tools.} If $n=xa+yb+zc$ with integers $x,y,z\ge0$, then $ye^2\equiv-n\pmod f$, and $e$ is
invertible modulo $f$. From this: $jb$ is a sum of edges only as $b+\dots+b$ for $1\le j<f$; a
representation of $jc$ with $j<f$ contains no $b$; and $ja$ is a sum of edges only as $a+\dots+a$ for
$1\le j<f$. The third fact replaces $ja<b$. At $j=f$ the first and the third fail, since
$fb=(f-e)(a+c)$ and $fa=ec$.
\item[3.] \emph{Run lemma.} The hypothesis at the right end $R$ of the run is: $R$ is a corner of angle
$\beta$ of $\Delta$, or the tile beside the last tile of the run across its side $a$ shows $\alpha$ at $R$,
so that it lays an edge $b$ or $c$ on the line of that side. This line bounds the lattice triangle on the
right. Its segment from $R$ to a lattice point consists of fewer than $f$ edges $a$ of lattice tiles and is
clamped at $R$; by the partition lemma and the third fact of step 1, a tile beyond the line has the lattice
point in the relative interior of an edge. For $e=1$ this was obtained from $ja<b$. In the induction the
same hypothesis is needed with $R$ replaced by a lattice point $Z$ of an inner line, and it then includes
that the line is clamped below $Z$; the clamp is the side $s$ or an edge of a lattice tile that ends at $Z$.
Wherever a wedge is filled, only the figures of Lemma~\ref{lem:figures} and the corresponding lists for the
angles $\alpha$, $\alpha+\beta$, $2\alpha$, $3\alpha$ and $\gamma$ are used; no inequality between $\alpha$
and $\beta$ and none between $a$ and $b$ is used.
\end{enumerate}
Steps 2, 4 and 5 are unchanged.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:ebelow}]
Let $(a,b,c)=(ef,f^2-e^2,f^2)$. If a side of integer length $n$ carries $x$, $y$, $z$ edges of lengths $a$,
$b$, $c$, then $n\equiv-ye^2\pmod f$. For $n=Me(2f^2-e^2)$ and for $n=Me(3f^2-e^2)$ we have
$n\equiv-Me^3\pmod f$; since $e$ is invertible modulo $f$, this gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:modfe}
y\equiv Me\pmod f .
\end{equation}

(a) Let $Me<f$ and suppose that $W_M(e,f)$ has a tiling. The side of length $n=Me(2f^2-e^2)$ lies opposite
the angle $2\alpha$, so its corners have the angles $\alpha+\beta$ and $\beta$. By \eqref{eq:modfe},
$y\ge Me$, because $0<Me<f$. Then $zf^2\le Me(2f^2-e^2)-Me(f^2-e^2)=Mef^2$ and $z\le Me<f$. But by
Theorem~\ref{thm:siderunall} this side carries at least $f$ tiles with their side $c$ on it.

(b) Let $2Me<f$ and suppose that $T_M(e,f)$ has a tiling. Both corners of the base have the angle $\beta$,
and its length is $n=Me(3f^2-e^2)$. As before $y\ge Me$, so $zf^2\le Me(3f^2-e^2)-Me(f^2-e^2)=2Mef^2$ and
$z\le2Me<f$, against Theorem~\ref{thm:siderunall}.
\end{proof}

For the members with $f\le40$ a count of the words, made by a program and not checked by the kernel, shows
that the two conditions are the limit of this argument: for $M<f$ some word on the side of part (a) has at
least $f$ letters $c$ when $Me\ge f$, and some word on the base when $2Me\ge f$.

Corollary~\ref{cor:thickw} uses the third side of $W_M(e,f)$, of length $Mfb$, which runs from the corner
$2\alpha$ to the corner $\alpha+\beta$. Theorem~\ref{thm:siderunall} does not apply to it. The statement
that takes its place has a second alternative.

\begin{proposition}[Sides from a corner $2\alpha$ or $3\alpha$; \sF]\label{prop:cornerrun}
Let $1\le e<f$ be coprime integers and $(a,b,c)=(ef,\,f^2-e^2,\,f^2)$. Let $s$ be a side of a triangle
$\Delta$ that is cut into copies of $(a,b,c)$, from a corner $A'$ of angle $2\alpha$ or $3\alpha$ to a
corner $Q'$ of angle $\beta$ or $\alpha+\beta$; place $s$ horizontally with $A'$ on the left and $\Delta$
above, and use the types of Section~\ref{sub:below}. Then one of the following holds.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(i)}] $s$ carries $f$ consecutive tiles of type $C_1$ or $f$ consecutive tiles of type $C_2$.
\item[\textup{(ii)}] The tiles on $s$ begin at $A'$ with exactly $k$ tiles of type $C_1$, where
$e+1\le k\le f-1$; the first of them and the tile beside it at $A'$ are mirror images in their common edge
of length $b$; and the $k$ tiles are followed by the corner $Q'$ or by a tile of type $A_2$, $B_2$ or $C_2$.
\end{enumerate}
If $a<b$ and $A'$ has the angle $2\alpha$, then $k\ge e+2$ in \textup{(ii)}. For the members $(f-1,f)$
alternative \textup{(ii)} is empty.
\end{proposition}

The proof first shows, by the rules of step 4, that $s$ carries no tile of
type $A_1$ or $B_1$ if all runs are shorter than $f$; so $s$ begins with a run of $k<f$ tiles of type $C_1$
whose right end satisfies the hypothesis of the run lemma. The tile beside the first tile at $A'$ shows
$\alpha$ there. If it lays its side $c$ along the first tile, the induction of the run lemma applies and
gives a contradiction. Otherwise it is the mirror image of the first tile, and a second induction over the
lattice triangle shows that this is impossible for $k\le e$. That induction uses that a row of fewer than $e$ lattice edges $c$ is covered from
above only by edges $c$; a row of $e$ edges $c$ can be covered by $f$ edges $a$, because $ec=fa$. For
$k=e+1$, $a<b$ and a corner $2\alpha$, the covering by edges $a$ is excluded because one of its tiles would
leave $\Delta$ across the second side at $A'$. Whether alternative \textup{(ii)} occurs at all is not known
to us.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:thickw}]
Let $(a,b,c)=(ef,f^2-e^2,f^2)$, and let the side of $W_M(e,f)$ of length $n=Mfb$ carry $x$, $y$, $z$ edges
of lengths $a$, $b$, $c$. It lies opposite the angle $\beta$, so it runs from the corner $2\alpha$ to the
corner $\alpha+\beta$. Since $n\equiv0\pmod f$, we have $y=mf$ with an integer $m\ge0$, and dividing
$n=xef+mfb+zf^2$ by $f$ gives $xe+zf=(M-m)b$. Put $M'=M-m$. If $M'=0$, then $z=0$. Let $M'\ge1$. Modulo $f$
the equation reads $xe\equiv-M'e^2$, so $x\equiv-M'e\equiv M'(f-e)$.

(a) Let $M(f-e)\le e$. Then $0<M'(f-e)\le e<f$, so $x\ge M'(f-e)$, and
$zf\le M'(f^2-e^2)-M'(f-e)e=M'(f-e)f$. Hence $z\le M'(f-e)\le e$: the side carries at most $e$ tiles with
their side $c$ on it. So it carries neither $f$ consecutive tiles of type $C_1$ or $C_2$ nor $e+1$ tiles of
type $C_1$, against Proposition~\ref{prop:cornerrun}.

(b) For $M\le f-1$ this is part (a) with $e=f-1$, where $M(f-e)=M\le e$. For $M\ge f$ a tiling exists by
Theorem~\ref{thm:existence}.

(c) Here $(a,b,c)=(15,16,25)$ and $n=160$. The solutions of $15x+16y+25z=160$ with $5\mid y$ are
$(x,y,z)=(9,0,1)$, $(4,0,4)$, $(2,5,2)$ and $(0,10,0)$, so $z\le4$. Since $a<b$ and the side starts at the
corner $2\alpha$, Proposition~\ref{prop:cornerrun} requires $5$ consecutive tiles with their side $c$ on it,
or an initial run of $k\ge e+2=5$ tiles of type $C_1$.
\end{proof}

Parts of these corollaries were known. At $M=1$ they give no new statement: $T_1(e,f)$ and $W_1(e,f)$ have
no tiling by Theorem~\ref{thm:scaleone}. The pairs $W_2(2,3)$, $W_2(3,4)$, $W_2(5,6)$ and $W_2(2,5)$ were
excluded before by search certificates, in the formal exclusions of $56$, $92$, $188$ and $184$
(Section~\ref{sub:excl}); for them the corollaries are a second argument. The scale-one statements were
proved first by Paliy \cite{PaliyPrime}. The ledger \cite{PaliyLedger}
states that every side of a tiled $W_M(e,f)$ or $T_M(e,f)$ carries two whole edges of length $f^2$, and for
$W_2(f-1,f)$ it records the forced word $c,c,a,a$ on the side $Mfb$. We have not compared the proofs, and we
make no claim of priority.

All arguments of this subsection concern one side of the target, and they exclude a target only when one of
its sides is too short to carry $f$ edges $c$. For $W_M(e,f)$ the two sides give $Me<f$ and $M(f-e)\le e$;
for $T_M(e,f)$ the legs are long enough at every scale and the base gives $2Me<f$. For the remaining pairs
every side admits a word with $f$ consecutive letters $c$, and an exclusion has to use two sides together or
the interior of the target.

\subsection{The layer}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:layer}]
In this proof $M$ denotes the scale of the triangle that contains the layer (the scale $M'$ of the statement), so
that part \textup{(a)} reads: if $2M\ge f+2$ and $T_{M-1}(1,f)$ has a tiling, so has $T_M(1,f)$.
Let $(a,b,c)=(f,f^2-1,f^2)$. The height of $T_1(1,f)$ is $H_1=f^3\sin\beta$, which is $f$ times the height
$c\sin\beta$ of the tile over its side $a=f$. Hence a trapezoid of height $H_1$ with parallel sides $u$ and
$v$, or a triangle of height $H_1$ over a side $u$ (with $v=0$), has the area of $u+v$ tiles. All pieces
below have the height $H_1$. From left to right along the base, the layer consists of:
\begin{itemize}
\item the tile at scale $f$, with its side $fa$ on the base and its side $fc$ on the left leg; it has $f^2$
tiles, and its third side, of length $fb$, gives the direction of the cuts between the following pieces;
\item $x_a=(2M-f-2)f+1$ columns, each made of $f$ parallelograms with a side $a$ parallel to the base and a
side $b$ in the direction of the cuts; a column has width $a$ and $2f$ tiles;
\item $M$ columns, each made of $b$ parallelograms with a side $b$ parallel to the base and a side $a$ in
the direction of the cuts; a column has width $b$ and $2b$ tiles;
\item the quadrilateral of six pieces of the collar of Theorem~\ref{thm:existence} for $e=1$, which is a
trapezoid with bottom $fc+(f-1)a$, top $(f-1)b$, left side $fb$ in the direction of the cuts and right side
$fc$ on the right leg; it has $fc+(f-1)a+(f-1)b=1+2fb$ tiles.
\end{itemize}
Consecutive pieces share a segment of length $fb$, since $f$ sides $b$ and $b$ sides $a$ both give
$fb=ba$. The condition $x_a\ge0$ is $2M\ge f+2$. The widths on the base add up to
\[
fa+x_aa+Mb+fc+(f-1)a=\bigl(1+(2M-f-2)+M+f+1\bigr)f^2-M=M(3f^2-1),
\]
the base of $T_M(1,f)$, and the widths on the upper line to $x_aa+Mb+(f-1)b=(M-1)(3f^2-1)$. The numbers of
tiles add up to
\[
f^2+2fx_a+2bM+1+2fb=6Mf^2-3f^2-2M+1=(2M-1)(3f^2-1),
\]
which is $M(3f^2-1)+(M-1)(3f^2-1)$, the number that the area of the layer requires. The part of $T_M(1,f)$
above the layer is a translate of $T_{M-1}(1,f)$.

(a) If $2M\ge f+2$, a tiling of $T_{M-1}(1,f)$ and the layer give a tiling of $T_M(1,f)$. If $2M\le f+1$,
then $2(M-1)\le f$ and $T_{M-1}(1,f)$ has no tiling by Corollary~\ref{cor:below}(b). (b) By (a) the set of
scales with a tiling is closed under $M\mapsto M+1$; it contains $f$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:existence} and no
$M$ with $2M\le f$. (c) follows from (b).
\end{proof}

The formal proof packs the same pieces: a translate of $T_{M'-1}$, the
parallelograms, the six pieces of the trapezoid and the tile at scale $f$.
The trapezoid of six pieces is Paliy's. The proposition also shows where an answer to
Question~\ref{q:threshold} for $e=1$ cannot come from: for $2M\ge f+2$ the part of $T_M(1,f)$ below the
height $H_1$ has a tiling with a straight upper side, so no argument confined to that part excludes
$T_M(1,f)$.

\subsection{Every member: a signed decomposition, a layer, and a strip of $W_M(e,f)$}\label{sub:layerall}
Both propositions are formal; part \textup{(b)} of Proposition~\ref{prop:layerall} and part \textup{(a)} of
Proposition~\ref{prop:wstrip} are stated in the form of the formal statements, which assert that the pieces lie
in the trapezoid with disjoint interiors and, with a tiling of the smaller triangle, form a tiling of the
larger one. The propositions are constructions and implications between tilings, and they decide no instance.
A \emph{wall} of a tiling is a segment that contains no interior
point of a tile.

\begin{proposition}[Every member: signed decomposition and layer; \sF]\label{prop:layerall}
Let $1\le e<f$ be coprime integers, $(a,b,c)=(ef,\,f^2-e^2,\,f^2)$, and $M\ge1$. Let $T_M(e,f)=ABC$ with apex
$A$ and base $BC$, and let $H_1$ be the height of $T_1(e,f)$ over its base.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(a)}] Let $Q'$, $Q$ be the points of the base with $|BQ'|=|CQ|=Mef^2$, and let $Z$ be the point of
the ray $AQ$ beyond $Q$ with $|QZ|=Me^2f$. Then $ABQ'$, $ACQ$ and $AQ'Z$ are copies of the tile at scale $fM$;
the triangle $Q'QZ$ is a copy of the tile at scale $Me$ and lies on the other side of the base; and
$T_M(e,f)\cup Q'QZ=ABQ'\cup AQ'Z\cup ACQ$, with disjoint interiors on each side. The triangle $ABQ$ is a copy
of $W_M(e,f)$, and $ABQ\cup Q'QZ=ABQ'\cup AQ'Z$.
\item[\textup{(b)}] If $2f(M-e)\ge b$, the part of $T_M(e,f)$ above the line parallel to the base at the height
$eH_1$ is a translate of $T_{M-e}(e,f)$, and the trapezoid below that line contains pieces with disjoint interiors,
cut into $(2Me-e^2)(3f^2-e^2)$ copies of the tile in all, which together with a tiling of that translate form a
tiling of $T_M(e,f)$ in which the line is a wall.
\item[\textup{(c)}] If $2fM\ge b$ and $T_M(e,f)$ has a tiling, so has $T_{M+e}(e,f)$. If $2fM\ge b$ and
$k\ge1$, then $T_M(e,f)$ has a tiling if and only if some tiling of $T_{M+ke}(e,f)$ has the chord parallel to
the base at the height $keH_1$ as a wall.
\end{enumerate}
For the members $(f-1,f)$ the condition of \textup{(b)} is $M\ge f$.
\end{proposition}

For $e=1$ part \textup{(b)} is the layer of Proposition~\ref{prop:layer}. For the members $(f-1,f)$ the layer
exists only at the scales $M\ge f$, so parts \textup{(b)} and \textup{(c)} relate no two scales below $f$. For
the other members with $e\ge2$ two scales below $f$ are related only when $(f-e)^2\ge2f$.

\emph{The idea.} For \textup{(a)}: two copies of the tile at scale $fM$ fit into the base corners of
$T_M(e,f)$, with their sides $fMc$ on the legs, and between them an isosceles triangle with the angle $\alpha$
at $A$ remains. A third copy at scale $fM$, laid against one of the two, covers this triangle and exceeds it
beyond the base by a copy of the tile at scale $Me$; the lengths agree because $b+e^2=c$. So the target is
three scaled tiles less one. A scaled tile is cut into tiles by the lines parallel to its sides. Hence a
quantity that is additive over the tiles, or an invariant of the boundary with values in a group, takes on
$T_M(e,f)$ the value it takes on this signed family of tiles, at every scale, and so does not exclude
$T_M(e,f)$ at any scale; the same holds for $W_M(e,f)$. Unlike Proposition~\ref{prop:blind}, this does not
use the tilings at the scales $M\ge f$. An argument that uses an inequality satisfied by each tile is not
covered by it. For \textup{(b)}: the layer
is a row of pieces of one height, cut from one another by parallel lines. At one base corner lies the tile at
scale $ef$. Then come $2Mf-(f^2+2ef-e^2)$ columns of $ef$ parallelograms with a side $a$ on the base, and $eM$
columns of $b$ parallelograms with a side $b$ on the base; each parallelogram is two tiles, and the slant side
of either kind of column has the length $efb$. At the other corner lies a trapezoid of six pieces: the four
triangles and the two parallelograms of the collar in step 2 of the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:existence}. The
condition $2f(M-e)\ge b$ says that the first number of columns is not negative. With these pieces the height
cannot be less than $eH_1$: at a height $gH_1$ with $g<e$ a column with the side $b$ on the base would consist
of $gb/e$ parallelograms, and $b$ is prime to $e$. Part \textup{(c)} follows by stacking layers, since the
upper side of a layer is straight.

\begin{proposition}[Every member: a strip of $W_M(e,f)$, and the scale $f-1$; \sF]\label{prop:wstrip}
Let $1\le e<f$ be coprime integers and $(a,b,c)=(ef,\,f^2-e^2,\,f^2)$. For $M\ge2$ let $s$ be the side of
length $Mfb$ of $W_M(e,f)$, which joins the corners $2\alpha$ and $\alpha+\beta$, and let $s'$ be the chord
parallel to $s$ that cuts off a translate of $W_{M-1}(e,f)$ at the corner $\beta$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(a)}] Let $M\ge2$, and let $z$ be an integer with $z\equiv Mf\pmod e$ and $f\le z\le Mb/f$. Then
the trapezoid between $s$ and $s'$ is the union, with disjoint interiors, of lattice pieces each cut into copies of
the tile, $(2M-1)(2f^2-e^2)$ in all; so a tiling of $W_{M-1}(e,f)$ gives one of $W_M(e,f)$ in which $s'$ is a wall.
Such an integer $z$ exists if and only if $M(f-e)\ge f$.
\item[\textup{(b)}] If $M(f-e)\ge f$ and $W_{M-1}(e,f)$ has a tiling, then $W_M(e,f)$ has a tiling.
\item[\textup{(c)}] $W_M(e,f)$ has no tiling for every $M<f$ if and only if $W_{f-1}(e,f)$ has none.
\item[\textup{(d)}] $W_{f-1}(e,f)$ has a tiling if and only if some tiling of $W_f(e,f)$ has the chord $s'$ as
a wall.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

Part \textup{(c)} is a reduction in one direction. If $W_{f-1}(e,f)$ has no tiling, then no smaller scale has
one; a tiling of $W_{f-1}(e,f)$ would decide nothing about the smaller scales. Part \textup{(c)} uses
Corollary~\ref{cor:thickw}\textup{(a)}, which is formal. For the members $(1,f)$ and $(f-1,f)$
the triangles $W_M(e,f)$ are settled (Corollaries~\ref{cor:below} and~\ref{cor:thickw}), so parts \textup{(c)}
and \textup{(d)} are new for $2\le e\le f-2$. The first targets $W_{f-1}(e,f)$ of these members are $W_4(2,5)$,
with $736$ tiles, and $W_4(3,5)$, with $656$; both are open.

\emph{The idea.} The strip consists of five pieces, each a union of triangles of a lattice tiling. On $s$,
from the corner $2\alpha$, lie $z$ tiles with their side $c$ on $s$ and then $x=(Mb-zf)/e$ tiles with their
side $a$ on $s$, so that $zc+xa=Mfb$. Above the first group are the $f$ lowest rows of the lattice tiling of
the tile at scale $z$; above the second group are $x$ columns of $e$ parallelograms with sides $a$ and $c$.
The two groups fit because $fa=ec$: in one direction, $f$ edges $a$ and $e$ edges $c$ have the same length. A
tile at scale $f$, reflected in the bisector of the corner $2\alpha$, and a tile at scale $e$ at the corner
$\alpha+\beta$ close the two ends, and a parallelogram of $(M-1)f$ columns of $f$ parallelograms with sides
$b$ and $a$ fills the rest up to $s'$. The $f$ rows require $z\ge f$. The count of edges on $s$ in the proof
of Corollary~\ref{cor:thickw} gives $z\le M(f-e)$ when $M(f-e)<f$, so that no such $z$ exists then; and when
$M(f-e)\ge f$ the interval from $f$ to $Mb/f$ contains $e$ consecutive integers. For \textup{(c)}: if
$W_M(e,f)$ has a tiling at a scale $M<f$, then $M(f-e)\ge e+1$ by Corollary~\ref{cor:thickw}\textup{(a)},
hence $(M+1)(f-e)\ge f+1$, and \textup{(b)} applies at every larger scale up to $f-1$.

The trapezoid of six pieces in the layer is Paliy's, as for $e=1$, and the tilings at the scales $M\ge f$, to
which Proposition~\ref{prop:layerall}\textup{(c)} and Proposition~\ref{prop:wstrip}\textup{(d)} refer, are
Paliy's and Beeson's (Theorem~\ref{thm:existence}). Paliy's collar passes from $W_T(e,f)$ to $W_{T+e}(e,f)$
along the side of length $Me(2f^2-e^2)$. The strip of Proposition~\ref{prop:wstrip}, along the side of length
$Mfb$ and in steps of one, was not found in the ledger \cite{PaliyLedger} as read; this is an absence in one
unrefereed source, and we make no claim of priority.

\subsection{What is known on Question~\ref{q:threshold}}

\begin{proposition}[Reduction and complete members; \sF]\label{prop:finite}
Let $1\le e<f$ be coprime. Then $\tau(e,f,M)$ holds for $M=1$ and for every $M\ge f$, and it holds for every
$M\ge1$ if and only if $T_M(e,f)$ has no tiling for $2\le M<f$. Moreover:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(i)}] $\tau(1,2,M)$, $\tau(1,3,M)$ and $\tau(2,3,M)$ hold for every $M\ge1$;
\item[\textup{(ii)}] $\tau(1,4,M)$ and $\tau(3,4,M)$ hold for every $M\ne3$;
\item[\textup{(iii)}] $\tau(1,f,M)$ holds whenever $2M\le f$ or $M=2$ or $M\ge f$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The first sentence is Theorems~\ref{thm:scaleone} and~\ref{thm:existence}. The rest follows with
Proposition~\ref{prop:scale2} and Corollary~\ref{cor:below}.
\end{proof}

By Corollary~\ref{cor:ebelow}\textup{(b)}, $\tau(e,f,M)$ also holds whenever $2Me<f$; this is formal as well.

\begin{proposition}[Quantities determined backwards; \sF]\label{prop:blind}
Let $1\le e<f$ be coprime, let $\iota$ be a function on the positive integers with values in a set $X$, and
let $z\in X$ be such that $\iota(M)=z$ whenever $T_M(e,f)$ has a tiling. Suppose that $\iota(M)=z$ follows
from $\iota(M')=z$ for all $M'>M$. Then $\iota(M)=z$ for every $M$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:existence}, $\iota(M)=z$ for $M\ge f$; induct downwards.
\end{proof}

The hypothesis holds for a periodic $\iota$, for an $\iota$ with values in an abelian group and a vanishing
iterated difference, and for an $\iota$ that satisfies a linear recurrence which can be solved for its first
term; these three cases are Lean lemmas. Counts modulo an integer, additive functions of the edge vectors,
areas, and the images of the boundary word of $T_M(e,f)$ in finite, nilpotent or linear quotients of the
group generated by the edge steps modulo the boundaries of the tiles, in the sense of Conway and Lagarias
\cite{ConwayLagarias}, are of these three kinds; we do not write out this verification here, and it is not formalized. Such a quantity therefore takes the same value at
$M=1$, where there is no tiling, as at the scales with a tiling, and cannot answer
Question~\ref{q:threshold}. An answer has to use the order of the tiles, as the proofs of
Theorems~\ref{thm:scaleone} and~\ref{thm:siderun} do.

\section{What is open}\label{sec:open}

We list what a determination of the set of tile counts still requires. We make no estimate of how far these
items are from being settled.

\subsection{An outside check of the prime case}
Theorems~\ref{thm:primes} and~\ref{thm:scaleone} are formal theorems whose statements have not been
checked by an independent reader (Section~\ref{sub:formalprime}). Such a reader has to compare the definitions of
Section~\ref{sub:leandefs} and the two formal statements with the mathematical ones; a continuous written
proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:scaleone} for $e\ge2$ does not exist. The exclusions of composite $N$ that use
Theorem~\ref{thm:scaleone} (Sections~\ref{sub:excl} and~\ref{sub:infinite}) inherit this.

\subsection{All $N$}
No description of the set of tile counts above $200$ is proved or conjectured here. For each $N$ the
classification leaves finitely many pairs $(\Delta,T)$ with explicit sides, and the question is which of
them have tilings. No family of pairs can be excluded as a whole, since each of the families below contains
pairs with tilings. By type:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Angles in $\Q\pi$; $\Delta$ similar to $T$; a right angle.} Settled in the literature: these
give the classical families \cite{BeesonSeven,BeesonI,SWW}.
\item \emph{Type \textup{(D)}.} $N$ is not squarefree \cite[Thm.~11.7]{BeesonIso}. For the values of
Section~\ref{sec:200} the remaining pairs of this type were excluded one at a time; no general statement is
known to us.
\item \emph{Types \textup{(S)} and \textup{(H)} on the equilateral triangle.} By
Corollary~\ref{cor:nonsquare} only tiles with integer sides remain, finitely many for each $N$. Which of
them tile is open; Laczkovich relates the possible tiles to rational points on elliptic curves
\cite{LaczkovichEll}. For the tile $(3,5,7)$ the sides $15$, $30$ and $45$ have no tiling and the side $60$ has
one.
\item \emph{Type \textup{(H)} on the other targets.} The number of tiles is $m^2N_0$ with $N_0$ given by
Propositions~\ref{prop:forms} and~\ref{prop:spectrum}. Zhang \cite{Zhang} constructs tilings for every
sufficiently large $m$ in certain residue classes; his constructions on the equilateral, isosceles and $F_1$ targets are proved here for every tile with an angle $2\pi/3$ at once: if $t_0ab=a^2+b^2+ia+jb$ with $i,j\ge0$, then $abm^2$, $a(b+2a)m^2$, $b(a+b)m^2$ and their mirror images are tile counts for every $m\ge3t_0$ (\lean{ZhangEqui.zhang_equi_m}, \lean{ZhangProps.zhang_five}), and the ledger \cite{PaliyLedger} announces further multipliers. For small
$m$ both outcomes occur: $143$ on $F_2$ and $88$ on $F_4$ are realized for the tile $(3,5,7)$, and $990$
on $F_3$ for the tile $(8,7,13)$, the triangle $(169,286,315)$, by a tiling of Paliy \cite{PaliyTilings}
that is kernel-checked here (\lean{N990.tileCount_990}), and $330$ on $F_4$ for the tile $(7,8,13)$, the
triangle $(91,176,195)$, by an explicit tiling found by exact search and kernel-checked here
(\lean{N330.tileCount_330}); the ledger \cite{PaliyLedger} announces this row for every multiplier, and for $a<b$ Paliy's five-piece construction (a scale-$c$ triangle, three scale-$a$ triangles and a $(b-a)\times a$ parallelogram) is proved here for every tile, giving $(2a+b)(a+b)$ as a tile count whenever $0<a<b$ and $c^2=a^2+ab+b^2$ (\lean{F4All.f4_family}); for $a<b$ the row F3 likewise, by Paliy's construction (the same layout with an added trapezoid), gives $3(a+b)(a+2b)$ as a tile count (\lean{F3All.f3_family}); the remaining rows (F3 with $a>b$, reversed F4, F2) of the ledger were rebuilt in exact arithmetic from Paliy's descriptions and checked for every member up to $10^6$ (a computation, not formal), as was his tiling of $(961,1426,1155)$ by $4830$ copies of $(24,11,31)$; while the
pairs of this type for $N=33$, $105$, $120$, $132$, $154$ and $184$ have no tiling, these $N$ not being tile
counts. A criterion is not known to us.
\item \emph{Type \textup{(T)}, the targets $T_M(e,f)$.} Question~\ref{q:threshold}. A tiling exists for
$M\ge f$ and none for $M=1$; for $e=1$ there is none when $2M\le f$; for every member there is none when
$2Me<f$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:ebelow}); these statements are formal. Open are the scales $M\ge2$ with
$f/(2e)<M<f$, apart from $T_2(1,3)$, $T_2(2,3)$ and $T_2(3,4)$, which have no tiling
(Proposition~\ref{prop:scale2}). In the
order of $f$ the first open instances are $T_3(1,4)$ and, for $e\ge2$, $T_3(3,4)$. The exclusions above scale
one that are known for infinitely many members all come from a count on one side of the target, and at the
open scales that count gives nothing, as far as it has been computed (Section~\ref{sub:allmembers}).
For $e=1$ the open scales reduce to the single scale $M=f-1$ for each $f\ge4$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:layer}), and
two further statements, which are theorems of the Lean library (\lean{SignedDecompE1.signed_decomposition},
\lean{ReframeBWall.reframe_b_wall}, \lean{ReframeC.reframe_c}), delimit the methods. First,
$T_M(1,f)$ together with one copy of the tile at scale $M$ attached below its base is the union of three copies of
the tile at scale $fM$; hence no quantity that is additive over the tiles and no invariant with values in a group
distinguishes any scale, and an exclusion has to use that every point is covered once. Second, $T_{f-1}(1,f)$ has a
tiling if and only if some tiling of $T_f(1,f)$ has the chord at the height of $T_1$ as a wall, and it has none if
every tiling of $T_f(1,f)$ has a wall from the apex along a cevian of length at least $(f-1)f(f^2-1)$; the tilings of
$T_f(1,f)$ exist, so this form of the question can be tested.
For $e\ge2$ the first of these two statements holds as well, with the attached copy at scale $Me$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:layerall}, formal), but the reduction to a single scale does not. The layer of
Proposition~\ref{prop:layerall} has the height of $T_e(e,f)$ and relates the scales $M$ and $M+e$; it relates
two scales below $f$ only for the members with $(f-e)^2\ge2f$. For the members $(f-1,f)$ it exists only at the
scales $M\ge f$, so their open scales are not reduced: each scale $2\le M<f$ is a separate case, apart from
$T_2(2,3)$ and $T_2(3,4)$, and the first open one is $T_3(3,4)$.
\item \emph{Type \textup{(T)}, the targets $W_M(e,f)$.} A tiling exists for $M\ge f$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:existence}). For the members $(1,f)$ there is none for $M<f$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:below},
formal), and for the members $(f-1,f)$ there is none for $M<f$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:thickw}, formal); so these
members are settled. For the other members there is no tiling when $Me<f$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:ebelow}), none when
$M(f-e)\le e$, and none of $W_2(3,5)$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:thickw}), all formal. The remaining scales $M<f$ are open; they belong to the members with
$2\le e\le f-2$, and the first are $W_3(2,5)$, $W_4(2,5)$, $W_3(3,5)$ and $W_4(3,5)$. By
Proposition~\ref{prop:wstrip} (formal) the open part is, for each of these members, the single target
$W_{f-1}(e,f)$, in the direction of exclusion: if $W_{f-1}(e,f)$ has no tiling, then $W_M(e,f)$ has none for
any $M<f$, while a tiling of $W_{f-1}(e,f)$ would leave the smaller scales undecided. The first of these
targets are $W_4(2,5)$, with $736$ tiles, and $W_4(3,5)$, with $656$. The question for $W_{f-1}(e,f)$ is
equivalent to one about the tilings of $W_f(e,f)$, which exist: whether one of them has the chord that cuts
off a translate of $W_{f-1}(e,f)$ at the corner $\beta$ as a wall.
\item \emph{Type \textup{(T)}, the isosceles targets with base angles $\alpha+\beta$.} The triangle with
sides $tfb$, $tfb$, $teb$ has no tiling when $te<f$ or $(e,f,t)=(k,k+1,2)$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:atoms});
for the member $(2,3)$ the scale $t=8$ has a tiling, with $320$ tiles, kernel-checked
(\lean{S5N320.s5_8_2_3_tiling}). The other scales are open.
\item \emph{Type \textup{(T)}, the targets with angles $(\alpha,\alpha,\alpha+2\beta)$ and
$(2\alpha,\alpha,2\beta)$.} The number of tiles is $r^2(f^2-e^2)(2f^2-e^2)$ on the first
(\lean{BADL2.theoremA_L2_full}); on the second a layout gives $(2q^2-p^2)(3q^2-p^2)$ tiles for
coprime $0<p<q$ with $q^2>2p^2$ (\lean{S2G.s2_family_gen}), and a second layout, D.~Paliy's, gives the same
count for every $0<p<q$ without condition on $q^2-2p^2$ \cite{PaliyTilings}: five pieces meet at one point, the
tile scaled by $b$ twice, by $a$ and by $2q^2-p^2$, and a parallelogram of $2bp^2$ tiles. Its formal proof for all
$p<q$ is \lean{S2All.s2_family_all}; every inequality it needs has nonnegative coefficients in $(p,q-p)$. The case
$(p,q)=(3,4)$ is the $897$-tiling of $(256,368,273)$ by $(12,7,16)$ (\lean{N897.tileCount_897}). With the scaling of tilings, every
$k^2(2q^2-p^2)(3q^2-p^2)$, $k\ge1$, $0<p<q$, is a tile count.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Squarefree $N$}
For squarefree $N$ prime to $3$ three of the four odd residue classes modulo $8$ are reduced to explicit
forms (Theorem~\ref{thm:sqfree}); the class $1\pmod8$ and the multiples of $3$ are not treated. The values
$455$, and $517$, $2261$, $2485$, $3901$, are the first of the classes $7$ and $5$ modulo $8$ without a
verdict. Computation~\ref{comp:c3} counts the squarefree $N\le10^5$ that the present conditions do not
decide; some of them are tile counts, for instance $897$.

\subsection{Non-squarefree $N$}
For non-squarefree $N$ the decidable condition \lean{NSQMMain.not_tile_of_conditionsNSQ'}, with its leaf for the
targets $F_3$ sharpened to a square-discriminant test (\lean{NSQMMainZ.not_tile_of_conditionsNSQz}), excludes $N$ when, in
every branch of the classification, no scale whose square divides $N$ satisfies the count of that branch. Two further leaves
are sharpened in Lean. The leaf for the pairs of the equilateral target (the $\pi/3$ and $2\pi/3$ pairs) uses the
irrationality of the $\pi/3$ pairs with a non-square discriminant, for every $N$ and without a squarefree hypothesis
(\lean{E60Irr.e60Pair_of_not_isSquare}, \lean{NSQMMainZE.not_tile_of_conditionsNSQzE}); and the leaf of the cevian targets
$W$ uses Corollary~\ref{cor:thickw}(a) in addition to Corollary~\ref{cor:ebelow}(a)
(\lean{NSQRWT.rwStatement_nsqT}, \lean{NSQMMainZET.not_tile_of_conditionsNSQzET}; the previous release had
Corollary~\ref{cor:thickw}(a) as a hand-proved statement outside the count). We evaluated this condition, sharpened by
Corollary~\ref{cor:ebelow} on the base-$\beta$ branch, by the bound $te\ge2f$
of the $\theta$ targets (\lean{ThetaKill.theta_te_ge}), and by the exclusion of the pairs $(2,2(2k+1))$ in the
branch $\gamma=2\alpha$ (\lean{GLIFinal.leafG_of_dissection}), on all non-squarefree $N\le10^6$ (a computation; the conditions are formal).
The known families are those of Theorem~\ref{thm:200} and the formal families $S_2$, $F_4$ and $F_3$ above, scaled by squares.
Of the $392\,074$ such $N$, $71\,039$ are excluded by the formal conditions, $102\,471$ are tile counts by the known families
(including the families of this section) and $218\,564$ are undecided; the three sets are disjoint, as checked on the
lists of values. Of the $71\,039$ excluded values, $62\,150$ are excluded by the single Lean theorem
\lean{NSQMMainZET.not_tile_of_conditionsNSQzET}; the other $8\,889$ are excluded by the conditions together with the
sharpenings on the base-$\beta$ branch (Corollary~\ref{cor:ebelow}(b), $2Me<f$) and on the branch $\gamma=2\alpha$, each of
which is a formal statement, but their combination with the other leaves is made by the computation and not by one Lean
theorem. Of the $218\,564$ undecided values, $201\,952$ fail the branch $\gamma=2\alpha$ and for $74\,697$ this branch is the only
obstruction; the others are not excluded by it. For a value failing the branch, the tiles $(u^2,v^2-u^2,uv)$ give
$N=g^2(v^2-u^2)$ with $g\ge2$, the case $g=1$ being excluded, and for $g\ge2$ the side words of the isosceles target with
base angles $\alpha$ exist, so only a geometric argument can exclude these values.

This branch does have tilings beyond the known ones for the tile $(4,5,6)$ ($u=2$, $v=3$, at $g=15$ and $g=183$).
Laczkovich's decomposition of the $183$-tiling into seven triangles similar to the tile and one parallelogram
closes for every $(u,v)$ with $u<v<2u$ and $(v^2-u^2)^3v^2>u^8$: with $b=v^2-u^2$ and $g_0=v(u^4-u^2v^2+v^4)$, the number
$b\,(2buv\,g_0)^2$ is a tile count (\lean{LaczFamily.lacz_family_uncond}); for $(u,v)=(3,5)$ this is a first tiling of
the branch with $u\ge3$. In all tilings of the branch known to us no side carries an edge of length $a=u^2$.

\begin{theorem}[Herdt's macro-dissection, uniform in the tile; \sF]\label{thm:herdt}
Let $u<v<2u$ be coprime with $(v^2-u^2)^3v^2>u^8$, $b=v^2-u^2$, and $\mathrm{Vp}=b^3v^2-u^8$. With
$g_0=2v\bigl(\mathrm{Vp}+(u^4+buv)(u^4+bv^2)\bigr)$, the number $b\,(s g_0)^2$ is a tile count of the tile
$(u^2,v^2-u^2,uv)$ for every $s\ge1$ (\lean{HerdtFamily.herdt_uniform}; the finer form \lean{HerdtFamily.herdt_family}).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Sketch]
The isosceles target with base angles $\alpha$ is cut into seven triangles similar to the tile and four parallelograms
filled with rows of $b\times c$ bricks, as in B.~Herdt's $1125$-tiling by $(4,5,6)$ (the instance $(u,v)=(2,3)$, published
by Beeson \cite{BeesonIso}). The side lengths of the pieces are linear in the scale; the existence of positive integer
multiplicities for the pieces is two linear Diophantine equations whose solvability is exactly the inequality above.
\end{proof}
The instances $(u,v)=(3,5)$ ($N=19\,600$) and $(4,7)$ ($N=130\,977$) are tilings of the branch at scales far below those
of Laczkovich's construction above; the control $(2,3)$ gives $N=1125$, Herdt's tiling. The general family is ours;
the instance and its publication are Herdt's and Beeson's. This decides no new value of the census $N\le10^6$.

\begin{conjecture}\label{conj:aprime}
In every dissection of the isosceles triangle with base angles $\alpha$ into copies of the tile
$(u^2,v^2-u^2,uv)$, some leg carries no edge of length $u^2$; consequently $g\ge v$.
\end{conjecture}

\noindent If $g<v$, a word on a leg that contains an edge $c$ contains an edge $a$, so the conjecture gives $g\ge v$; it
holds on every tiling known to us and on all small instances searched, and it would decide about $199\,000$ of the
undecided $N\le10^6$ above. Conjecture~\ref{conj:aprime} is proved for $v-u=1$ and the case $g=2$ is proved for every pair, by Theorem~\ref{thm:Dprime} below (Section~\ref{sub:aprimehand}; written proof, not formalized); the case $d\ge2$, $g\ge\lceil u/d\rceil+1$ is open.

\begin{remark}[The equilateral triangle of side $ab$]\label{rem:eqgate}
For a tile $(a,b,c)$ with an angle $2\pi/3$, the isosceles target with legs $bc$ and base $b(a+2b)$ is the union of two
copies of the tile scaled by $b$ and the equilateral triangle of side $ab$, and every undecided squarefree instance on
an equilateral target has side $ab$ and $ab$ tiles. Whether the equilateral triangle of side $ab$ can be cut into $ab$
copies of the tile therefore decides, for squarefree $N\le10^6$, about $490$ of the undecided values in the isosceles
and equilateral branches together. For $(3,5,7)$ the sides $15$, $30$, $45$ are not tiled and $60$ is.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The target with base angles $\alpha$: exclusions proved by hand, not formalized}\label{sub:aprimehand}

This subsection is new in this release. Every statement in it is marked \sW: it has a written proof, which has been read
adversarially a second time within this work (two audit notes, one for Theorem~\ref{thm:C}, one for Lemma~\ref{lem:K} and
Theorem~\ref{thm:Dprime}; verdict: sound at the level of hand proofs), and none of it is formalized. Nothing in it enters
the formal counts of this paper, and no outside reader has checked it. Two ingredients are Lean theorems (the identity
used in Step~\ref{step:q1} below, and the parametrisation of the pairs, for which a draft exists that has not been
compiled and is therefore not claimed).

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Method and credit.} The method is that of Beeson and Ro\c{s}u \cite{BeesonRosu}, who prove the
prime case for the targets $(2\alpha,\beta,\alpha+\beta)$ and $(\beta,\beta,3\alpha)$ by an induction on the rows of a
\emph{quadratic tiling} built on a run of equal edges, which is fuelled by the \emph{rigidity window}: a segment shorter than
the minimal relation among the edge lengths has only the pure decomposition into edges. Their paper credits G.~Ro\c{s}u and
AI assistance. We do not use their results, only the idea. What is ours is the transfer to the tile with $\gamma=2\alpha$:
the fan lemma at straight points (which replaces the inequality $\gamma>\pi/2$ that their proofs use), the separation of the
rigidity window into the two numbers $u$ and $v$, the form of their Lemma~34 that we call Lemma~\ref{lem:34}, and
Lemma~\ref{lem:K}, which uses the window $v$ at the base corners. The proofs of Lemmas~\ref{lem:33}--\ref{lem:34} follow
the corresponding proofs of \cite{BeesonRosu} step by step, and we say below where a step changes.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Setting.} Let $u<v<2u$ be coprime, $d=v-u$, and let the tile have sides $a=u^2$, $b=v^2-u^2$,
$c=uv$ and angles $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ opposite $a,b,c$, so that $\gamma=2\alpha$, $\beta=\pi-3\alpha$ and
$\cos\alpha=v/2u$. Let $T(g)$ be the isosceles triangle with base angles $\alpha$, legs $gub$ and base $gvb$, so that a tiling has
$N=g^2b$ tiles; $g\ge1$ is an integer. For the pairs that occur in the leaf of the branch $\gamma=2\alpha$ this is no
restriction: a pair $(M,M')$ with $MM'=N$, $3M<M'$ and $M\equiv M'\pmod 2$ gives $g=\gcd(M'-M,M'+M)/2\in\Z$,
$M=gd$, $M'=g(2u+d)$, $\gcd(u,u+d)=1$, $0<d<u$ and $N=g^2b$, and conversely. (This is elementary; the Lean draft
\lean{Draft/ThmCPair.lean} of it has not been compiled.) The edges $a$, $b$, $c$ have their ends marked by the angles
$(\beta,\gamma)$, $(\alpha,\gamma)$, $(\alpha,\beta)$. A \emph{c-run} on a side $S$ of $T(g)$ is a maximal set of consecutive
edges $c$ on $S$. A run is \emph{stopped} at an end $V$ if $V$ is a vertex of $T(g)$, or the next tile along $S$ has its angle $\gamma$ at $V$.

\begin{lemma}[Fan lemma; \sW]\label{lem:fan}
$p\alpha+q\beta+r\gamma=\pi$ with $p,q,r\in\N$ if and only if $(p,q,r)\in\{(3,1,0),(1,1,1)\}$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Since $\cos\alpha=v/2u\in(\tfrac12,1)$ is rational, $\alpha\notin\Q\pi$ (Niven). Now $p\alpha+q\beta+r\gamma=
(p-3q+2r)\alpha+q\pi$, which equals $\pi$ only if $q=1$ and $p+2r=3$.
\end{proof}
Hence at a point of a side of $T(g)$ other than a vertex there is exactly one angle $\beta$ and at most one $\gamma$; the
angles $\alpha$ and $\beta$ of a tile are never cut; a sector $\gamma$ is a $\gamma$-tile or two $\alpha$-tiles; the sector
$\pi-\gamma$ is one $\alpha$-tile and one $\beta$-tile; the corners of $T(g)$ (angle $\alpha$) carry one tile, the apex (angle
$\pi-2\alpha=\alpha+\beta$) one tile with $\alpha$ and one with $\beta$; and no tile has $\gamma$ at a vertex of $T(g)$. We
use only these consequences.

\begin{lemma}[Rigidity; \sW]\label{lem:rigid}
The triples $(x,y,z)\in\Z^3$ with $xa+yb+zc=0$ are $(y'u+vs,\;uy',\;-us-y'v)$, $y',s\in\Z$. Consequently, for a segment of
length $n\cdot a$, $n\cdot b$ or $n\cdot c$ cut into edges $a,b,c$: \textup{(RA)} $na$ is cut into $n$ edges $a$ if $n\le v-1$;
\textup{(RB)} $nb$ is cut into $n$ edges $b$ if $n\le u-1$; \textup{(RC)} $nc$ contains no edge $b$ if $n\le v-1$, and is cut into
$n$ edges $c$ if $n\le u-1$. The values $n=v$ for $a$ and $c$ and $n=u$ for $b$ are attained: $va=uc$, $ub=d(a+c)$, $vc=u(a+b)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
From $xu^2+y(v^2-u^2)+zuv=0$ and $\gcd(u,v^2-u^2)=1$ we get $u\mid y$; put $y=uy'$. Dividing by $u$ gives
$u(x-y'u)=-v(y'v+z)$, so $x-y'u=vs$ and $z=-us-y'v$. A decomposition $na=x'a+yb+zc$ gives the relation $(x'-n,y,z)$ with $x',y,z\ge0$, so $y'\ge0$. If $y'\ge1$ then $z\ge0$ gives $s\le-y'v/u<-y'$, so $-s\ge y'+1$ and $n=x'-y'u-vs\ge v(y'+1)-y'u>v$; if $y'=0$ and $s\ne0$ then $s<0$ and $n=x'-vs\ge v$.
The statements (RB) and (RC) follow in the same way from the relations $(x,y-n,z)$ and $(x,y,z-n)$. (The same
statements were verified by exact computation for all coprime $u<70$.)
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Side words; \sW]\label{lem:words}
If a leg $gub$ is cut into $x$ edges $a$, $y$ edges $b$, $z$ edges $c$, then $(x,y,z)=(md+jv,\;u(g-m),\;md-ju)$ with
$0\le m\le g$, $j\in\Z$ and $md+jv\ge0$, $md-ju\ge0$. If the base $gvb$ is cut so, then $(x,y,z)=(Md+jv,\;gv-Mu,\;Md-ju)$
with $0\le M\le\lfloor gv/u\rfloor$ and $j$ subject to the same inequalities.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Lemma~\ref{lem:rigid} applied to the relation $(x,y-gu,z)$, resp.\ $(x,y-gv,z)$, with the substitution $s=m+j$, resp.\ $s=M+j$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Stopped runs are long, away from the corners; \sW]\label{lem:33}
Let $U$ be a point of a side $S$ of $T(g)$, not a vertex, and $T_1$ a tile with its angle $\alpha$ at $U$, its edge $c$ on $S$ and
its edge $b$ on a ray $UW$ into the triangle. Suppose that a tile with a vertex at $U$, on the other side of $UW$ from $T_1$, has
an edge $a$ or $c$ on $UW$. Then the run of $c$-edges that starts at $U$ with $T_1$ and has $n\le u-1$ edges is not stopped at its far end.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof (that of {\cite[Lemmas~32 and~33]{BeesonRosu}}, with the changes noted)]
Take coordinates $x(i,j)=U+ic\,\hat\imath+jb\,\hat\jmath$, with $\hat\imath$ along $S$ and $\hat\jmath$ along $UW$. Let $\Delta_{i,j}$ be
the translate of $T_1$ by $x(i,j)$ and $\nabla_{i,j}$ its image under the half-turn about the midpoint of its edge $a$
(so that $\Delta_{i,j}\cup\nabla_{i,j}$ is a parallelogram with sides $b$ and $c$). Let $\mathcal T(s,r)$ be the trapezoid with
vertices $U,\,x(s,0),\,x(s-r,r),\,x(0,r)$ cut into the $\Delta_{i,j}$ and $\nabla_{i,j}$ it contains, and $H(w)$ the statement that
$\mathcal T(w,w)$ occurs in the dissection; $H(1)$ is the tile $T_1$. Suppose the run has $n\le u-1$ edges and is stopped at $V=x(n,0)$.
The induction shows that $H(w)$ implies $w<n$ and $H(w+1)$ for $1\le w\le n$; for $w=n$ this is absurd. The step $H(w)\Rightarrow H(w+1)$ is:
the ray $UW$ stays a wall beyond $P=x(0,w)$, because $wb$ has no decomposition other than into edges $b$ (RB, $w<u$) (the hypothesis on the tile across $UW$ is what makes $UW$ a wall at its start, as in \cite{BeesonRosu}); the diagonal $PQ$, $Q=x(w,0)$, has length $wa$ and no decomposition other than into $w$ edges $a$ (RA), so the tiles on its far side
form a column of $\nabla$'s, whose orientation is forced from the end $\beta$ at $P$ (two $\gamma$ at a point are excluded by Lemma~\ref{lem:fan}); the sectors that remain at
the lattice points of $PQ$ are filled by translates $\Delta$, the only alternative, an edge $b$ running along a row from a lattice point, being excluded by the trapezoid argument of
\cite[Lemma~32]{BeesonRosu}, which uses (RA), (RC) and that the points involved are interior to $T(g)$ (they lie on rays that are never along a side). At $Q$ the stop gives the contradiction:
if the tile next on $S$ has its $\gamma$ at $V$ it meets the $\gamma$ of $\nabla_{n-1,0}$, two $\gamma$ at a point; $V$ is not a vertex of $T(g)$ for the same reason.
The only change from \cite{BeesonRosu} is that every use of $\gamma>\pi/2$ there is at a straight point, where Lemma~\ref{lem:fan} replaces it, so that no regime assumption on $(u,v)$ is needed.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Runs starting at a base corner; \sW]\label{lem:K}
Let $g\ge2$, let $O$ be a base corner of $T(g)$ with its tile $T_1$ having the edge $c$ on one side $S$ and the edge $b$ on the other side $S'$.
A run of $n$ edges $c$ on $S$ starting at $O$ is not stopped at its far end if $n\le v-1$. Equivalently: a $c$-run on $S$ starting at a corner and stopped at both ends
has at least $v$ edges.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:33} is repeated with $U=O$ and one change. The ray $UW$ is now the side $S'$ of
$T(g)$, a wall without any hypothesis on a tile across; and for $w\le v-1$ the point $P=x(0,w)$ is interior to $S'$ ($wb<vb<2ub\le gub$, and $vb\le gvb/2$, for $g\ge2$), so $S'$
continues beyond $P$. The rigidity of $wb$ (window $u$) is therefore not needed, and what remains are the rigidity (RA) of $wa$ and (RC) of the lengths $(s-r-i_0)c<nc$, whose windows are $v$.
So the induction runs up to $w=v-1$, and a corner whose far vertex is a base corner of $T(g)$ is excluded outright since the angle left there cannot be filled.
\end{proof}
\begin{remark}
The window $v$ is sharp for this argument: at $n=v$ the diagonal $va=uc$ has a second decomposition. In the $19\,600$-tiling of the branch, one leg starts at a
corner with a run of $3=u$ edges $c$, followed by $b$'s; that run is not stopped. We use of Lemma~\ref{lem:K} only the stopped statement. The unstopped half
(that $\mathcal T(n,n)$ occurs and that the side $S'$ then begins with $\min(n,v)$ edges $b$) is not proved by the argument above, which closes its induction
at $w=n$ with the stop, and is neither used nor claimed (audit gap G1).
\end{remark}

\begin{lemma}[A side carries a long run stopped at both ends; \sW]\label{lem:34}
Let $S$ be a side of $T(g)$. \textup{(i)} Some $c$-run on $S$ is stopped at both ends. \textup{(ii)} Suppose that whenever a tile has its
angle $\alpha$ at an end $V$ of $S$ with its edge $c$ on $S$, another tile with a vertex at $V$ has an edge $a$ or $c$ on the ray
along the edge $b$ of the first tile. Then every $c$-run on $S$ stopped at both ends has at least $u$ edges. \textup{(iii)} For $g\ge2$ and without that
hypothesis: every side of $T(g)$ carries a $c$-run stopped at both ends with at least $u$ edges, and with at least $v$ edges if it starts at a base corner.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
(i) Each edge $a$ or $b$ has exactly one end with $\gamma$, an edge $c$ has none, no two $\gamma$ meet at a point of $S$ and no $\gamma$ is at a vertex of $T(g)$. Consider a maximal stretch $e_1,\dots,e_k$ of edges $a,b$.
If $e_1$ has its $\gamma$ on the right, so does $e_2$ (the common point would carry two $\gamma$), and so on, so $e_k$ has its $\gamma$ at its right end. Hence either $e_1$ has $\gamma$ at its left end, which stops the run
on the left of the stretch (and is impossible at a vertex), or $e_k$ has $\gamma$ at its right end, which stops the run on the right. If there is no $c$-edge on $S$ at all, the same argument puts a $\gamma$ at a vertex. So with $s\ge1$ runs
there are $s+1$ gaps (before the first run, between runs, after the last; an empty gap at an end of $S$ is a vertex, which stops the adjacent run by definition), each gap stops an end of an adjacent run, and the $s+1$ stopped ends are distinct. If every run had at most one stopped end there would be at most $s$.

(ii), (iii) Let $R$ be a run stopped at both ends. Each of its edges has its ends $\alpha,\beta$ in one of two orders, and $\beta|\beta$ does not occur, so $R$ reads $(\beta\alpha)^k(\alpha\beta)^l$. If $k,l\ge1$ there is a junction $\alpha|\alpha$;
its sector is $\pi-2\alpha=\alpha+\beta$, which contains a $\beta$-tile whose edge on the ray along the $b$-edge of the tile on its side is $a$ or $c$ (the edges at $\beta$); applying Lemma~\ref{lem:33} to the part of $R$ on that side of the junction,
which is stopped at its far end, gives $|R|\ge u$. If $k=0$, $R$ starts with $\alpha$ at its left end $X$ (the case $l=0$ is the mirror image). If $X$ is stopped by a $\gamma$-tile, the fan at $X$ is $\gamma,\beta,\alpha$ and the $\beta$-tile is across the ray along the $b$-edge of the first tile of $R$:
again Lemma~\ref{lem:33}. If $X$ is the apex, the hypothesis of (ii) (or, in (iii), the property of the apex: one $\alpha$-tile, one $\beta$-tile, sharing the ray) provides the tile across. If $X$ is a base corner, the hypothesis of (ii) is violated (the corner carries one tile), so
in (ii) $R$ does not start there; in (iii) Lemma~\ref{lem:K} gives $|R|\ge v\ge u$. Part (iii) follows from (i) and these cases.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[\sW; proved by hand, not formalized]\label{thm:C}
Let $u<v<2u$ be coprime, $d=v-u$, and $g\ge1$ an integer with $gd<u$ (equivalently $\lfloor gv/u\rfloor\, d<u$). Then $T(g)$ has no tiling by the tile $(u^2,v^2-u^2,uv)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For $g=1$ this is known (Beeson \cite[Thm.~11.7]{BeesonIso}; \lean{GPairArith}); let $g\ge2$. If $gd<u$ then $gv/u<g+1$, so $m\le g$ and $M\le g$ in Lemma~\ref{lem:words}, hence $md,Md<u<v$, which forces $j=0$ and $z=md$ resp.\ $Md$: every
word of a leg and of the base has fewer than $u$ edges $c$. The two corner tiles of $T(g)$ have the edges $b$ and $c$ at their angle $\alpha$. If one of them has $b$ on its leg, then the hypothesis of Lemma~\ref{lem:34}(ii) holds on that leg (vacuously at that corner, and by the property of the apex at the other end),
so the leg carries a $c$-run of at least $u$ edges: impossible. Hence both have $c$ on the leg and $b$ on the base; then the hypothesis holds on the base at both ends, and the base carries a $c$-run of at least $u$ edges: impossible.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[\sW; proved by hand, not formalized]\label{thm:Dprime}
Let $u<v<2u$ be coprime, $d=v-u$, and $g\ge2$ an integer with $(g-1)(v-u)<u$. Then $T(g)$ has no tiling by the tile $(u^2,v^2-u^2,uv)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Step 1.}\label{step:q1} Let $A$ be the leg whose end at the apex carries the angle $\alpha$ of a tile; by Lemma~\ref{lem:fan} exactly one leg does. The counting of the angles $\beta$ along a leg (\lean{APrimeLeg.RealLeg.counting_identity}, a Lean theorem about the chain of tiles along a leg, which we use in the form: the number $q$ of edges $b$ on $A$ is at least $1$)
shows that $A$ has an edge $b$. So $q=u(g-m)\ge1$, that is $m\le g-1$, in the word of Lemma~\ref{lem:words}.
\emph{Step 2.} Then $md\le(g-1)d<u<v$, hence $j=0$ and the word of $A$ is $(md,\,u(g-m),\,md)$ with $md<u$ edges $c$.
\emph{Step 3.} By Lemma~\ref{lem:34}(iii) the leg $A$ carries a $c$-run with at least $u$ edges. This contradicts Step~2.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[\sW]\label{cor:W2}
\textup{(a)} $T(2)$ has no tiling by any tile $(u^2,v^2-u^2,uv)$ with $u<v<2u$ coprime: this is the statement for every integer $g=2$ (the pairs $W_2$). \textup{(b)} If $v-u=1$, then $T(g)$ has no tiling for $2\le g\le u$, that is for every $g<v$:
Conjecture~\ref{conj:aprime} holds for $d=1$. \textup{(c)} Theorem~\ref{thm:C} is the case $gd<u$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:Dprime}; the latter adds exactly the value $g=\lceil u/d\rceil$ for each pair with $d\ge2$, and $g=u$ for $d=1$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
(a) $(g-1)d=d<u$. (b) $(g-1)\cdot1<u$ if and only if $g\le u$. (c) $gd<u$ implies $(g-1)d<u$, and $(g-1)d<u\le gd$ for $g=\lceil u/d\rceil$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Decidability}

\begin{proposition}[\sW]\label{prop:decidable}
It is decidable, given $N$, whether $N$ is a tile count.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For fixed $N$ the existence of a triangle $P_1P_2P_3$ and of $N$ triangles $A_iB_iC_i$ that form a
dissection of it into congruent triangles is a sentence in the first-order language of ordered fields, with
the coordinates of the $3N+3$ points as variables: non-degeneracy is the non-vanishing of determinants;
congruence to $A_1B_1C_1$ is the equality of the three squared sides in some order; a point lies in a
triangle, or inside it, according to the signs of three determinants; the sentence says that no point lies
inside two of the triangles $A_iB_iC_i$ and that a point lies in $P_1P_2P_3$ if and only if it lies in some
$A_iB_iC_i$. The sentence is computable from $N$, and the first-order theory of the real field is decidable
\cite{Tarski}.
\end{proof}

This gives no bound that could be used and no description of the set. In Lean explicit criteria exist
on two domains only: the primes (Theorem~\ref{thm:primes}) and $N\le200$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:200}, a
conjunction of formal theorems, Section~\ref{sub:excl}).

\section{The formalization}\label{sec:formal}

\subsection{The definitions}\label{sub:leandefs}
The library is written in Lean~4 (version 4.30.0) with Mathlib and is in the directory
\texttt{lean/Erdos634/} of the repository. Its basic definitions are, in mathematical notation:
\begin{itemize}
\item \texttt{Tri}: three points of the Euclidean plane $\R^2$ that are affinely independent; its carrier
is their convex hull.
\item \texttt{Dissection N}: a target \texttt{Tri}, tiles indexed by $\{0,\dots,N-1\}$, the union of the
carriers of the tiles equal to the carrier of the target, and the interiors of the carriers of distinct
tiles disjoint.
\item \texttt{Tri.Congruent T U}: there are an isometry $g$ of the plane and a permutation $\sigma$ of the
indices with $g(T_k)=U_{\sigma(k)}$ for the three vertices.
\item \texttt{CongruentDissection N}: a \texttt{Dissection N} with a further \texttt{Tri}, the model, to
which every tile is congruent.
\item \texttt{TileCount N}: there is a \texttt{CongruentDissection N}.
\end{itemize}
These are Definition~\ref{def:tiling}. They are in the files \lean{Dissection.lean} and
\lean{Congruence.lean}, and \texttt{TileCount} is in \lean{Draft/TileCountFamilies.lean}. A statement about a
target or a tile with given sides is expressed by \texttt{HasSides T a b c}: the three sides of $T$ are
$a,b,c$ in some order of the vertices.

\subsection{Certificates}\label{sub:certs}
``Machine-checked'' has one meaning in this paper: the Lean kernel has checked a proof term of the
statement. Two kinds of generated data occur.

A \emph{tiling certificate} is a list of $N$ triangles with coordinates in $\frac1d\Z[\sqrt K]$. The kernel
evaluates, in exact arithmetic, that each triangle has the squared sides of the tile, that its vertices lie
in the closed target, that for each pair of triangles the line of an edge of one separates them, and that
the areas add up to the area of the target. A theorem of the library turns these four facts into a
\lean{CongruentDissection}.

A \emph{search certificate} proves that a pair $(\Delta,T)$ has no tiling. It is a finite tree. A node is a
partial configuration, given by the tiles placed so far, with a convex corner of the uncovered region and
one of the two boundary rays there; its children are the six placements of a tile with a vertex at that
corner and an edge along that ray, and a child is dead when the tile leaves the target or overlaps a placed
tile. That every tiling extending the configuration contains one of these placements is a theorem of the
library, also
for regions that are not triangles, and so is the soundness of the checker
(\lean{Refl} and \lean{ReflS} in \texttt{lean/Erdos634/}). The tree is proposed by a floating-point search,
which is not trusted: the tree is replayed in exact arithmetic over $\Q(\sqrt K)$ into Lean terms, and the
kernel checks every node. The largest certificate used here, for $N=135$, has $1\,326\,256$ nodes
(Theorem~\ref{thm:n135}); the others have at most $345\,746$. Five certificates of the library use in addition the conjugation
$\sqrt K\mapsto-\sqrt K$ of a tiling, which is Laczkovich's method of conjugate tilings \cite{Laczkovich2020};
the pairs that they exclude are also excluded by Corollary~\ref{cor:nonsquare}.

\subsection{Aggregates}
The library is built in aggregates that do not import one another, for reasons of memory:
\lean{Erdos634.All}, every module whose import closure contains no search certificate, among them the
whole proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:primes}; \lean{Erdos634.AllSpectrum}, the modules that depend on a search
certificate, with the statements on the values up to $200$; \lean{Erdos634.AllThreshold}, the results of
Section~\ref{sec:row}; and \lean{AllCert1} to \lean{AllCert5} for the seven largest certificates. These seven
are not stored in the repository; they are regenerated from a manifest that records the search parameters
and a hash of each file. In the library, three counting statements that are not used in this paper are
proved with \texttt{native\_decide}; every declaration cited here is recorded with the three standard
axioms only. The axioms of a declaration are listed by \texttt{\#print axioms}; Appendix~\ref{sub:check}
says how to rebuild the aggregates and check them.

\subsection{Checking the formal proofs}\label{sub:check}
The library builds with Lean~4 version 4.30.0 and Mathlib at the tag \texttt{v4.30.0}, as pinned in
\texttt{lean/lakefile.toml} and \texttt{lean/lean-toolchain}. A reader checks a numbered statement by building
the aggregate named in Table~\ref{tab:map} and printing the axioms of its declarations. The aggregates are to
be built one at a time: a single \texttt{lean} process needs up to about $13.5$~GB of memory for the largest
modules. The aggregate \lean{All} contains the whole proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:primes} and no search
certificate. The seven certificates used by \lean{AllCert1} to \lean{AllCert5} are not stored in the
repository; the script \texttt{code/regen\_cert.sh} regenerates each of them from the manifest
\texttt{data/certificates/MANIFEST.tsv}, by rerunning the search and the exact replay, and compares the
SHA-256 hashes of the search tree and of the generated Lean files with those of the manifest. The kernel check
of the largest of them, for $N=135$, takes several hours.
