\begin{abstract}
Fix coprime integers $2\le e\le f-2$ and the triangle (the \emph{tile}) with sides $ef,\ f^2-e^2,\ f^2$. The triangles $W_M$ and $T_M$ of scale $M$ were known to be tiled by $M^2(2f^2-e^2)$ and $M^2(3f^2-e^2)$ tiles when $M\ge f$ (Paliy). We prove that $W_M$ is tiled as soon as $Me\ge f$ and $(M-1)(f-e)\ge f$, equivalently $(f-e)\lfloor eM/f\rfloor\ge e+1$; for $T_M$ the tile count is proved and the tiling follows from a gluing lemma imported from the earlier release. The proof is one layout of six pieces in a skew frame with integer coordinates; it has a written proof of about four pages and, independently, a Lean~4 proof. For $e<f/2$ and \emph{assuming an imported exclusion theorem}, the condition is exact for $W_M$. Example: $W_4(3,5)$ is tiled by $656$ tiles (open in the earlier release). A program count (computed, not proved) gives $26\,138$ tile counts that were undecided in this project's census (not checked against Paliy's ledger). The paper says which part is Lean, hand, computed or imported.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{The result}
A triangle $\Omega$ is \emph{tiled by $N$ copies of a triangle $\tile$} if $\Omega$ is the union of $N$ triangles with pairwise disjoint interiors, each congruent to $\tile$ (mirror images allowed, no edge-to-edge condition).
An integer $N$ is a \emph{tile count} if some triangle is tiled by $N$ copies of some triangle; Erd\H{o}s asked which integers are tile counts (Problem~634 of \texttt{erdosproblems.com}).
Paper~W constructs tilings; nothing about primes is used or claimed (the tiles with $3\alpha+2\beta=\pi$ are the ones on which the base-$\beta$ approach of Beeson--Ro\c{s}u and Paliy to the prime case rests; here each tiling of a target is only a new tile count).

For coprime integers $1\le e<f$ put $a=ef$, $b=f^2-e^2$, $c=f^2$; the triangle $\tile(e,f)$ with sides $a,b,c$ has angles $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ opposite $a,b,c$ and $3\alpha+2\beta=\pi$.
Beeson--Ro\c{s}u and Paliy define two target triangles, $W_M(e,f)$ and $T_M(e,f)$, for each integer scale $M\ge1$ (Section~\ref{sub:targets}); their area is $M^2(2f^2-e^2)$ resp.\ $M^2(3f^2-e^2)$ times the area of the tile.
Before this paper both were known to be tiled for every $M\ge f$ (Paliy), and for $e\ge2$ the expectation was that no smaller $M$ works.
\begin{quote}
\textbf{Theorem~W} (Theorem~\ref{thm:W}). If $2\le e\le f-2$, $\gcd(e,f)=1$, $Me\ge f$ and $(M-1)(f-e)\ge f$, then $W_M(e,f)$ is tiled by $M^2(2f^2-e^2)$ copies of $\tile(e,f)$ and $T_M(e,f)$ by $M^2(3f^2-e^2)$.
\end{quote}
Example: $(e,f)=(2,5)$, $M=3$ gives a tiling of a triangle with sides $315,276,375$ by $414$ copies of $(10,21,25)$; before, the smallest scale known was $M=f=5$.
\emph{What this does not do.} It does not settle which $M$ fail when $e>f/2$ (Section~\ref{sec:open}), the members $e=1$ and $e=f-1$, or the prime case of Problem~634.

\subsection{Reader's guide}
\emph{The proof in outline} (Section~\ref{sec:proof}; every claim below is an equation or inequality a reader can check by hand; ``scale $s$'' means a triangle with sides $sa,sb,sc$, and a ``tile-row'' is a strip one scale-one tile wide).
(1) Put the origin at the point $P$ of the side $AB$ with $OP=OB$, and use as axes the lines $PO$ and $PB$ (Section~\ref{sub:frame}); in this skew frame the three vertices of $W_M$ and all other points below have \emph{integer} coordinates, and a vector $(\xi,y)$ has squared length $\xi^2+y^2+\tfrac ef\xi y$.
(2) Five directions occur; for each, one integer vector and its length are listed (Table~\ref{tab:dirs}), so every side length is read off as ``integer $\times$ length of a direction vector''.
(3) The line $OP$ cuts $W_M$ into the triangle $OAP$, which is a tile of the integer scale $fM$, and a triangle $OPB$.
(4) The lines $\ell_k$ ($k\in\mathbb Z$), parallel to a side of the tile, form a lattice; $\ell_t$ and $\ell_r$ are the two lattice lines on either side of $B$, and between them lies a strip $S$ one tile-row wide.
(5) Two cuts parallel to $OB$ divide $S$ into three pieces; with $\ell_t$, $\ell_r$ and the line $P_2P_3$ they give six pieces, defined by inequalities.
(6) Covering is a case distinction on the signs of $y$, $u-t$, $u-r$ and $w-q$; disjointness is: two pieces lie on opposite sides of one of six lines (Section~\ref{sub:cover}).
(7) Each piece is a triangle, a trapezoid or a parallelogram of integer scale; Table~\ref{tab:sides} lists its side vectors (Section~\ref{sub:shapes}).
(8) Everything above needs only $ft<eM<fr$, $er<fq$, $eq<ft$ for the integer parameters $t,r,q$ (Section~\ref{sub:K}); these four inequalities have a solution exactly when $(f-e)\lfloor eM/f\rfloor\ge e+1$, equivalently when $Me\ge f$ and $(M-1)(f-e)\ge f$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:equiv}, Proposition~\ref{prop:exact}).
(9) The tile counts of the six pieces add up to $M^2(2f^2-e^2)$ (one identity).

\emph{How the layout was found.} A computer search (our engine, run on the $M<f$ cases left open in the earlier release) produced tilings whose pieces were always the same six shapes; the proof below says why: the bisector $OP$ makes one big tile, and what is left near $B$ is a strip that one row of lattice-line pieces fills.\par\smallskip\emph{What is proved where.}

\subsection{The tile and the ladder}\label{sub:tile}
Throughout, $e<f$ are coprime positive integers and
\[
a=ef,\qquad b=f^2-e^2,\qquad c=f^2,\qquad \kappa=b+c=2f^2-e^2,\qquad d=f-e .
\]
The \emph{tile} $\tile(e,f)$ has sides $a,b,c$ (a triangle since $a+b-c=ed>0$); $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ are its angles opposite $a,b,c$. One checks $c^2=a^2+bc$, and $3\alpha+2\beta=\pi$, $\gamma=2\alpha+\beta$. The only angle used below is
\[
\cos\beta=\frac{a^2+c^2-b^2}{2ac}=\frac{e(3f^2-e^2)}{2f^3}.
\]
Examples: $(e,f)=(2,5)$ gives the tile $(10,21,25)$; $(3,5)$ gives $(15,16,25)$; $(1,2)$ gives $(2,3,4)$.
\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figures/fig_tile.pdf}
\caption{The tile $\tile(e,f)$ for $(e,f)=(2,5)$, the triangle $(10,21,25)$: sides $a=ef$, $b=f^2-e^2$, $c=f^2$ and angles $\alpha,\beta,\gamma=2\alpha+\beta$ opposite them; $3\alpha+2\beta=\pi$. In tiling figures, tiles of the same orientation (up to a half-turn) share a colour and mirror images are hatched.}\label{fig:tile}
\end{figure}

A triangle with sides $sa,sb,sc$ has \emph{scale} $s$ (a copy of the tile enlarged by $s$; by SSS it is determined by its side lengths).
\begin{proposition}[Ladder subdivision; \PL{}]\label{prop:ladder}
Let $s,u\ge1$ be integers. A triangle of scale $s$ is tiled by $s^2$ copies of $\tile$. A parallelogram with sides $sa$ and $uc$ enclosing the angle $\beta$ is tiled by $2su$ copies.
\end{proposition}
\noindent\emph{Lean:} \lean{Subdivision.ladderDissection}, \lean{Ladder.ladder} (earlier release, \texttt{thm:ladder}). \emph{Why:} the lines parallel to the sides at integer steps cut a triangle of scale $s$ into $s^2$ triangles of scale one, all congruent to the tile (half of them rotated by $\pi$); a parallelogram with sides $a,c$ and angle $\beta$ is two tiles, since its short diagonal has length $b$ (law of cosines, $\cos\beta$ above), and a parallelogram with sides $sa,uc$ is $su$ of those.
A \emph{trapezoid} below is a triangle of scale $s_1$ minus a corner triangle of scale $s_2<s_1$ cut off by a line parallel to the opposite side; it is tiled by $s_1^2-s_2^2$ copies (the corner is the union of $s_2^2$ cells of the ladder).

\subsection{The frame}\label{sub:frame}
Let $P$ be the point of $AB$ with $|OP|=|OB|$. Then $|PA|=Mef^2$ and $|PB|=|AB|-|PA|=Me\kappa-Mef^2=Meb$ (so $P$ lies between $A$ and $B$ and $PB=ebM$, $PA=ef^2M$). In the isosceles triangle $OPB$ the angle at $P$ equals the angle at $B$, and $\cos\angle OPB=\frac{|PB|/2}{|OB|}=\frac{e}{2f}$.
\emph{The frame.} Use $P$ as origin, the unit vector from $P$ towards $O$ as first axis and the unit vector from $P$ towards $B$ as second axis. A point has coordinates $(\xi,y)$, and a vector $(\xi,y)$ has (law of cosines)
\begin{equation}\label{eq:metric}
\text{length}^2=\xi^2+y^2+\tfrac ef\,\xi y .
\end{equation}
Then $O=(fbM,0)$, $B=(0,ebM)$, $A=(0,-ef^2M)$, and (\ref{eq:metric}) gives $|OB|^2=b^2M^2(f^2+e^2-e^2)=(fbM)^2$, $|OA|^2=M^2f^2(b^2+e^2f^2+e^2b)=(f^3M)^2$ (because $b^2+e^2b+e^2f^2=f^4$), $|AB|=ef^2M+ebM=Me\kappa$: this is $W_M$.

\emph{Lattice coordinates.} Put $\varphi=f\xi+ey$ and $\psi=e\xi+fy$, and
\[
u=\frac{\varphi}{efb},\qquad w=\frac{\psi}{efb},\qquad\text{so that}\qquad \xi=ef(fu-ew),\quad y=ef(fw-eu).
\]
For an integer $k$ the line $\ell_k$ is $\{u=k\}$; it has direction $(e,-f)$. It meets $PO$ ($y=0$) at $\xi=ebk$ and $AB$ ($\xi=0$) at $y=fbk$. The side $OB$ is $\{w=M\}$, the line $AB$ is $\{\xi=0\}=\{ew=fu\}$, the line $OP$ is $\{y=0\}=\{eu=fw\}$.
$u,w$ are used only to say which side of a line a point lies on; lengths are always computed in $(\xi,y)$.

\section{Theorem W and its consequences}\label{sec:thmW}

\subsection{Statement}
\begin{theorem}[Theorem W; $W$ half \PL{} and \PH{}; count of $T_M$ \PL{}; tiling of $T_M$ via the imported glue]\label{thm:W}
Let $e,f$ be coprime integers with $2\le e\le f-2$ and let $M\ge1$ be an integer with
\[
\text{(H1)}\quad Me\ge f,\qquad\qquad \text{(H2)}\quad (M-1)(f-e)\ge f .
\]
Then $W_M(e,f)$ is tiled by $M^2(2f^2-e^2)$ copies of $\tile(e,f)$, and $T_M(e,f)$ is tiled by $M^2(3f^2-e^2)$ copies.
\end{theorem}
Put $M^*(e,f)=\max\bigl(\lceil f/e\rceil,\ \lceil f/(f-e)\rceil+1\bigr)$; then (H1) and (H2) together say $M\ge M^*(e,f)$.
\smallskip

\emph{Exactly what is Lean.} Lean module \texttt{Draft/ThmW.lean} (with 19 helper modules, aggregate \lean{Erdos634.AllThmW}), theorem \lean{Draft.ThmW.thmW}: for natural numbers $e,f,M$ with \texttt{Nat.Coprime e f}, $2\le e$, $e+2\le f$, $f\le M\cdot e$ and $f\le (M-1)\cdot(f-e)$, the conclusion is
\[\texttt{WTilable e f M} \ \wedge\ \texttt{TileCount ((2*f\^{}2-e\^{}2)*M\^{}2)}\ \wedge\ \texttt{TileCount (M\^{}2*(3*f\^{}2-e\^{}2))}.\]
\texttt{WTilable e f M} says: there is a dissection into congruent triangles, each congruent to the triangle with sides $ef,\ f^2-e^2,\ f^2$, of a triangle with sides $Mf(f^2-e^2)$, $Me(2f^2-e^2)$, $Mf^3$. So Lean states the \emph{tiling} for $W_M$; for $T_M$ it states only that the number is a tile count, and the tiling of $T_M$ itself is the glue (I5), a Lean theorem of the earlier release (Section~\ref{sub:T}). Axioms: \texttt{propext}, \texttt{Classical.choice}, \texttt{Quot.sound}. The Lean statement was compared with the one above inside the project (verdict: match). Hypotheses are witnessed by instances \lean{inst_414}, \lean{inst_656}, \lean{inst_801} (and others), so the Lean theorem is not vacuous.
\emph{The Lean proof does not follow Section~\ref{sec:proof}}: it verifies, for the layout in complex coordinates, a table of about $100$ cross products factored into positive factors, with $143$ generated sign lemmas (Appendix~\ref{app:complex}). Section~\ref{sec:proof} is a second proof of the $W$ half (the same layout, a different verification of it), written for a reader; it is not formalised.

The theorem has many more instances with $M<f$, for example $(e,f,M)=(4,9,3)$ and $(3,8,3)$.

\subsection{Consequences}
\begin{corollary}[Sharpness for $e<f/2$, assuming the import (I1); ``if'': Theorem~\ref{thm:W} \PL{} plus the arithmetic below; ``only if'': imported (I1)]\label{cor:sharp}
Let $\gcd(e,f)=1$ and $2\le e<f/2$. Then $W_M(e,f)$ is tiled if and only if $Me\ge f$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
``Only if'' is (I1) (Section~\ref{sub:import}). ``If'': $e<f/2$ gives $f-e>f/2$, so $f/(f-e)<2$ and $\lceil f/(f-e)\rceil+1=3$, while $f/e>2$ gives $\lceil f/e\rceil\ge3$; so $M^*=\lceil f/e\rceil$. Moreover (H1) implies (H2): (H1) gives $M\ge3$, so $(M-1)(f-e)\ge2(f-e)>f$. Apply Theorem~\ref{thm:W}.
\end{proof}
For $e<f/2$ the threshold is therefore \emph{exactly} $\lceil f/e\rceil$, smaller than $f$ as soon as $e\ge2$. (For $e=1$ it is $f$, by (I3).) The ``only if'' half is not proved in this paper; it is only as strong as the import (I1).
\begin{corollary}[An open case; \PL{} for the tiling, \CE{} for the data]\label{cor:W435}
$W_4(3,5)$, a triangle with sides $320,\,492,\,500$, is tiled by $656$ copies of $(15,16,25)$.
\end{corollary}
\noindent This is Theorem~\ref{thm:W} for $(e,f,M)=(3,5,4)$ ($M^*(3,5)=\max(2,4)=4$). A list of the $656$ tiles in exact coordinates is shipped (Appendix~\ref{app:data}) and verified by an exact checker.

\begin{remark}[An earlier expectation that is false]\label{rem:refute}
(a) \emph{The threshold conjecture ``B1''.} Our earlier notes expected that, for $e\ge2$, neither $W_M$ nor $T_M$ is tiled for $M<f$; the earlier paper \cite{ReleasePaper} recorded as an observation that every case decided so far has threshold $f$, and listed $W_4(3,5)$ among the open cases. Corollary~\ref{cor:W435} is a counterexample ($4<5$), and Theorem~\ref{thm:W} gives infinitely many (for each $e\ge2$ and $f$ large, $M=\lceil f/e\rceil<f$ works when $e<f/2$, by Corollary~\ref{cor:sharp}).
The first statement was an expectation of our working notes and an observation in the earlier paper, not a theorem; no earlier theorem is affected.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The $T$ half}\label{sub:T}
Glue to the side $Mfb$ of $W_M$ (the side $OB$) one copy of the tile at scale $Mf$, with its side $Mfb$ on that side and its third vertex $Z$ on the line $AB$ beyond $B$, so that $BZ=Mef^2$. The result is the isosceles triangle with legs $Mf^3$ and base $Me(3f^2-e^2)$, that is $T_M$, and its tile count is $M^2(2f^2-e^2)+(Mf)^2=M^2(3f^2-e^2)$.
This is (I5). The Lean theorem \lean{Draft.ThmW.thmW} contains the \emph{count} for $T_M$, obtained from the tiling of $W_M$ by \lean{DebtB.counts_of_wTilable}, which needs besides the tiling only $M(f-e)\ge f$; (H2) implies it.
The converse (a tiling of $T_M$ gives one of $W_M$) is not known, and the proved exclusions for $T_M$ are weaker ((I1): only $2Me<f$). Hence the label of the $T$ half: tiling of $W_M$ \PL{}\,\PH{}; tile count of $T_M$ \PL{}; tiling of $T_M$ by the imported glue \PL{} in the earlier release.

\subsection{Five facts imported from the earlier release}\label{sub:import}
Five facts about the tile and the targets are Lean theorems of the earlier release \cite{ReleasePaper,Release22} and are used as stated, not reproved. \emph{Tiled} means tiled by copies of $\tile(e,f)$ with the same $(e,f)$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(I1)] (\texttt{cor:ebelow}(a), (b), and the case $e=1$) If $Me<f$ then $W_M(e,f)$ is not tiled; if $2Me<f$ then $T_M(e,f)$ is not tiled; for $e=1$, $T_M(1,f)$ is not tiled if $2M\le f$. [All coprime $1\le e<f$.]
\item[(I2)] (\texttt{cor:thickw}(a)) If $M(f-e)\le e$ then $W_M(e,f)$ is not tiled.
\item[(I3)] (\texttt{cor:below}, \texttt{cor:thickw}(b)) For $e=1$ and for $e=f-1$: $W_M(e,f)$ is tiled if and only if $M\ge f$.
\item[(I4)] (Paliy; formalised there) For every $M\ge f$, $W_M(e,f)$ and $T_M(e,f)$ are tiled.
\item[(I5)] (the glue; Lean \lean{CollarG.betaTilable_of_wTilable} and \lean{DebtB.counts_of_wTilable}) If $W_M(e,f)$ is tiled then $T_M(e,f)$ is tiled; and if $W_{M_0}(e,f)$ is tiled and $M_0(f-e)\ge f$, then for every $M\ge M_0$ both $W_M$ and $T_M$ are tiled and $M^2(2f^2-e^2)$, $M^2(3f^2-e^2)$ are tile counts.
\end{enumerate}
They are used only outside the proof of the $W$ half: (I1) for Corollary~\ref{cor:sharp} and to say what is open; (I2), (I3) to say what is open and in Remark~\ref{rem:refute}; (I4) to place the theorem against what was known; (I5) for the $T$ half. The proof of the $W$ half (Section~\ref{sec:proof}) uses none of them.

\subsection{An equivalent form of the hypotheses}\label{sub:equiv}
Write $n=\lfloor eM/f\rfloor$, $\rho=eM-fn\in[0,f)$ and $\delta=1$ if $\rho=0$, $\delta=0$ otherwise.
\begin{lemma}[\PH{}; not in Lean]\label{lem:equiv}
Let $2\le e\le f-2$, $\gcd(e,f)=1$, $d=f-e$, $M\ge1$. Then (H1) and (H2) hold if and only if $d\,n\ge e+1$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
First, $ed\ge f+1$: with $f=e+d$ we have $ed-f=(e-1)(d-1)-1$, and $(e-1)(d-1)\ge2$ because $e,d\ge2$ and $e=d=2$ would give $f=4$, not coprime to $e=2$. $(\ast)$

($\Leftarrow$) Suppose $dn\ge e+1$. Then $n\ge1$, i.e.\ $eM\ge f$: (H1). Suppose (H2) failed: $(M-1)d\le f-1$. Then $dM\le f+d-1<2f$, so $s:=\lceil dM/f\rceil\in\{1,2\}$, and $n=\lfloor M-dM/f\rfloor=M-s$. If $s=1$ then $dM\le f$ and $dn=d(M-1)\le f-d=e$; if $s=2$ then $dn=d(M-2)\le f+d-1-2d=e-1$. Either way $dn\le e$, a contradiction.

($\Rightarrow$) Suppose (H1), (H2). If $d\ge e+1$: $n\ge1$ by (H1), so $dn\ge d\ge e+1$. If $d\le e$: $d\nmid f$ (else $d\mid e$, so $d=1$, i.e.\ $e=f-1$, excluded), so $f=dh+j$ with $0<j<d$, and $h\ge2$ because $f=e+d\ge2d$; then $e=f-d=d(h-1)+j$. (H2) gives $M-1\ge\lceil f/d\rceil=h+1$, so $M\ge h+2$, and
\[
e(h+2)-hf=e(h+2)-h(e+d)=2e-hd=d(h-2)+2j>0,
\]
so $eM\ge e(h+2)>hf$, hence $n\ge h$ and $dn\ge dh=f-j\ge f-d+1=e+1$.
\end{proof}
\begin{corollary}[Theorem~W, restated; \PH{} (Lemma~\ref{lem:equiv}) on top of \PL{}]\label{cor:equiv}
For coprime $2\le e\le f-2$ and $M\ge1$ with $(f-e)\lfloor eM/f\rfloor\ge e+1$, the target $W_M(e,f)$ is tiled by $M^2(2f^2-e^2)$ tiles and $T_M(e,f)$ by $M^2(3f^2-e^2)$ tiles. The tile counts of the six pieces of Section~\ref{sec:proof} are, for $\delta=0$,
\[
f^2M^2-e^2n^2,\quad (fM-en-e)^2,\quad f^2n^2,\quad \rho(2f-\rho),\quad 2f\,dn,\quad e(2dn-e).
\]
\end{corollary}
\noindent The hypotheses of the Lean theorem stay (H1), (H2); the lemma is proved here, by hand only. Consequently $M^*(e,f)$ is the least $M$ with $d\lfloor eM/f\rfloor\ge e+1$.

\section{Proof of the $W$ half of Theorem~W}\label{sec:proof}
Standing assumptions: $e,f$ coprime, $2\le e\le f-2$, $d=f-e\ge2$, $M\ge1$; $t,r,q$ integers.

\subsection{The four inequalities}\label{sub:K}
We use the following condition on $(t,r,q)$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:K}
\text{(K)}\qquad ft<eM<fr,\qquad er<fq,\qquad eq<ft .
\end{equation}
In lattice coordinates, (K) says: on the line $AB$ the point $B$ lies between the lattice lines $\ell_t$ and $\ell_r$, and on the lines $\ell_t,\ell_r$ the level $q$ lies in the window $er/f<q<ft/e$. Consequences used below (each a one-line derivation):
\begin{gather}
t<r,\quad t\ge1,\quad q\ge1,\quad q<M,\notag\\
e^2r<f^2t,\quad er<fM,\quad et<fq,\quad et<fM.\label{eq:consK}
\end{gather}
(Derivations: $ft<eM<fr$ gives $t<r$; $er>0$ and $er<fq$ give $q\ge1$; $eq<ft<eM$ gives $q<M$; $eq>0$ gives $t\ge1$; $e^2r<efq<f^2t$; $er<fq<fM$; $et<er<fq$; $et<eM<fM$.)
All six scales (\ref{eq:scales}) are positive and $s_1>s_2$, $u_1>u_2$: $v=fM-er>0$; $s_1=f(r-t)>0$; $s_2=fr-eM>0$; $s_1-s_2=eM-ft>0$; $x=ft-eq>0$; $u_2=fq-er>0$; $u_1-u_2=e(r-t)>0$.

\begin{proposition}[the layout exists exactly when $dn\ge e+1$; \PH{}, \CE{} for the enumeration]\label{prop:exact}
For coprime $2\le e\le f-2$ and $M\ge1$ the following are equivalent:
(i) there are integers $t,r,q$ with (K);
(ii) $d\,n\ge e+1$ where $n=\lfloor eM/f\rfloor$;
(iii) (H1) and (H2).
If they hold, the triple $t=n-\delta$, $r=n+1$, $q=n$ satisfies (K). Every triple satisfying (K) has $t\le n-\delta$ and $r\ge n+1$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(ii)$\Leftrightarrow$(iii) is Lemma~\ref{lem:equiv}. (ii)$\Rightarrow$(i) for the stated triple: $n\ge1$ by (ii). \emph{$ft<eM$:} if $\delta=0$ then $\rho>0$ and $fn<fn+\rho=eM$; if $\delta=1$ then $f(n-1)<fn=eM$. \emph{$eM<fr$:} $eM=fn+\rho<fn+f$. \emph{$er<fq$:} $e(n+1)<fn\Leftrightarrow dn>e$, which is (ii). \emph{$eq<ft$:} if $\delta=0$, $en<fn$; if $\delta=1$ then $f\mid eM$, so $f\mid M$ (coprimality), $M=fm'$, $n=em'$ and $dn\ge de\ge f+1$ by $(\ast)$, which is $en<f(n-1)$.
(i)$\Rightarrow$(ii) and the last sentence: from $ft<eM$ we get $t<eM/f<n+1$, so $t\le n$, and $t\le n-1$ if $\rho=0$; from $eM<fr$ we get $r>n$, so $r\ge n+1$. Suppose $dn\le e$, that is $fn\le e(n+1)$. Then $fq>er\ge e(n+1)\ge fn$, so $q\ge n+1$, and $eq\ge e(n+1)\ge fn\ge ft>eq$, a contradiction.
\end{proof}
\noindent So the layout below exists for \emph{no} integers $t,r,q$ when $dn\le e$; this is a statement about this layout, not about tilings (Section~\ref{sec:open}). \CE{}: for $f\le12$ and all $M<3f$, all integer triples with $0\le t<r\le fM/e+2$, $0\le q\le M+2$ were enumerated; (K) (and the sixteen named factors of the older proof, Appendix~\ref{app:complex}) hold for the same triples; no discrepancy. \begin{remark}[why these values of $t,r,q$]
Only $t\le n-\delta$ and $r\ge n+1$ are forced (by $ft<eM<fr$). The admissible triples are not unique: for $W_3(2,5)$ the triples $(t,r,q)=(1,2,1),(1,2,2),(1,3,2),(1,4,2)$ all satisfy (K). The canonical triple $(n-\delta,n+1,n)$ is the extremal admissible one: it has the narrowest strip, and it is admissible whenever any triple is (Proposition~\ref{prop:exact}). Nothing else is claimed.
\end{remark}

(b) $\pi_1$ and $\pi_2$ are triangles of scales $v$ and $ft$. (c) $RQV_2$ has scale $s_1$ and $RBB'$ is its corner at $R$ of scale $s_2$ ($B$ on $RQ$, $B'$ on $RV_2$, $BB'\parallel QV_2$, both $\parallel OB$): $\pi_3$ is a union of $s_1^2-s_2^2$ tiles. (d) $X_4U_1P_2$ has scale $u_1$ and $X_4C'U_2$ is its corner of scale $u_2$ ($C'U_2\parallel P_2U_1$): $\pi_5$ is a union of $u_1^2-u_2^2$ tiles. (e) $\pi_4$ has the sides $U_1-Q=fx\,(e,-f)$ and $V_2-Q=es_1\,(f,-e)$, of lengths $cx$ and $as_1$, and $U_2-V_2=U_1-Q$, $U_2-U_1=V_2-Q$, so it is a parallelogram; the angle at $Q$ between $(f,-e)$ and $(e,-f)$ has cosine
\[
\frac{\bigl\langle(f,-e),(e,-f)\bigr\rangle}{f\cdot f}=\frac{2ef+\frac{e}{2f}\bigl(-f^2-e^2\bigr)}{f^2}=\frac{e(3f^2-e^2)}{2f^3}=\cos\beta ,
\]
where $\langle(\xi,y),(\xi',y')\rangle=\xi\xi'+yy'+\frac{e}{2f}(\xi y'+y\xi')$ is the bilinear form of (\ref{eq:metric}); so the angle is $\beta$ and, by Proposition~\ref{prop:ladder}, $\pi_4$ is a union of $2s_1x$ tiles.

\subsection{Step 3: the count, and the end of the proof}\label{sub:count}
By Steps 1 and 2, $W_M$ is the union of six pieces with disjoint interiors, each a union of tiles with the counts of Table~\ref{tab:pieces}; so $W_M$ is tiled by their sum. That sum is $M^2\kappa$: the identity
\[
(f^2M^2-e^2t^2)+v^2+f^2t^2+(s_1^2-s_2^2)+2s_1x+(u_1^2-u_2^2)=M^2(2f^2-e^2)
\]
is a polynomial identity in $e,f,M,t,r,q$ (expand with (\ref{eq:scales}); checked by computer algebra, Appendix~\ref{app:checks}). It is also forced by area: the tiles are disjoint, cover $W_M$, and all have the area of the tile. For $\delta=0$ and the canonical triple it reads, with $eM=fn+\rho$, $f^2M^2-e^2n^2+(fM-en-e)^2+f^2n^2+\rho(2f-\rho)+2f\,dn+e(2dn-e)=M^2\kappa$.
The canonical triple of Proposition~\ref{prop:exact} satisfies (K) under (H1), (H2). This proves the $W$ half of Theorem~\ref{thm:W}. The $T$ half is Section~\ref{sub:T}. \hfill$\square$

\smallskip\noindent\emph{Remark} (where the hypotheses enter). (H1) gives $n\ge1$; (H2) enters only through $dn\ge e+1$, that is the single inequality $er<fq$ with $q=n$, $r=n+1$: the apex $X_4$ of the corner removed from $\pi_5$ lies on $PO$ at or beyond $C'$. Everything else in (K) holds for all $M\ge1$ with $dn\ge1$ (for $\delta=1$ the inequality $eq<ft$ needs $dn>f$, which follows from $de\ge f+1$ since $n=em'$).

\emph{Remark.} A shorter alternative to Step 1 (a winding-number chain lemma, audited inside the project, not in Lean) is given in Appendix~\ref{sub:chain}; it is not needed here.

\section{Consequences, and what stays open}\label{sec:open}
\subsection{Effect on the census of tile counts}
Let $F$ be the set of those numbers $M^2(2f^2-e^2)$ and $M^2(3f^2-e^2)$, for the triples $(e,f,M)$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:W}, that are non-squarefree and $\le10^6$. By the theorem all of them are tile counts. The census of the companion covers the non-squarefree integers $N$ with $2\le N\le10^6$ (the squarefree ones are treated separately there and are not part of this count). Among them the set $F$ has $44\,495$ elements ($28\,946$ of the form $W$, $18\,431$ of the form $T$, with $2\,882$ values of both forms; largest $f$ is $251$), none of them is excluded by the exclusion theorem of the earlier release, $18\,357$ were known tile counts by other constructions, and \textbf{$26\,138$ moved from ``undecided'' (neither known to be a tile count nor excluded by the exclusion theorem of the earlier release) to ``tile count''} (each such value is an instance of the Lean theorem \lean{thmW}, which is universal in $(e,f,M)$; the count $26\,138$ itself comes from a program, \CE{}, and the ``undecided'' status comes from the companion census). The census itself, the other constructions and the conjecture that organises them are in the companion paper \cite{Companion}.
Two examples: $414=2\cdot 3^2\cdot 23$ and $639=3^2\cdot71$ were undecided before ($414=W_3(2,5)$, $639=T_3(2,5)$).

\subsection{What stays open}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{$e<f/2$: nothing} (Corollary~\ref{cor:sharp}) for $W_M$; for $T_M$ the scales with $2Me\ge f$ and $M<M^*(e,f)$ are neither proved tiled nor excluded (for example $T_3(2,11)$).
\item \textbf{$e>f/2$, the ``no'' side.} The exclusion imported here is only (I2), $M(f-e)\le e$. So for each row $(e,f)$ with $e>f/2$ the scales $M$ neither covered by Theorem~\ref{thm:W} nor excluded by (I2) are exactly those with $e/d<M<f/d+1$, $d=f-e$ (for $e>f/2$ the condition $Me\ge f$ holds for all $M\ge2$); the interval has length exactly $2$, so it contains at most two integers: at most two undecided scales per row. (That the interval holds at most two integers is proved: it is an open interval of length $2$.) \CJ{}\ \emph{Conjecture $W^*$:} for coprime $e,f$ with $2\le e\le f-2$, $W_M(e,f)$ is tiled iff $M\ge M^*(e,f)$ (for $e>f/2$: iff $(M-1)(f-e)\ge f$).
Examples, relative to the imports (I1)--(I3) only (see also the next item): $W_2(3,5)$, $W_3(3,5)$, $W_2(5,8)$. For $W_2(5,8)$ ($412=4\cdot103$ tiles) a restricted exact computer search found no tiling (evidence only, not formal, \CE{}); the next item says it is decided by a newer theorem.
\item \textbf{A newer exclusion, not part of this release's claims.} In the project repository (not in the release that accompanies this paper; so here $W_2(3,5)$ and $W_2(5,8)$ are open) there is a Lean theorem, \lean{Draft.SplusThick.wTilable_not_of_small_M}, stating that $W_M(e,f)$ is not tiled when $\gcd(e,f)=1$, $2\le e<f$, $M<f$, $f\le Me$ and $M(f-e)\le e+1$; it extends (I2) by one and decides $W_2(3,5)$ and $W_2(5,8)$ (no tiling) and leaves $W_3(3,5)$ open. Its hand proof has been audited inside the project (verdict: sound, with one repair), the Lean file is marked unaudited in its header, and it has not been checked by anyone outside the project. It does not interact with Theorem~\ref{thm:W}: for $e>f/2$ Theorem~\ref{thm:W} needs $(M-1)(f-e)\ge f$, the exclusion needs $M(f-e)\le e+1<f$.
\item \textbf{$e=1$ and $e=f-1$} lie outside Theorem~\ref{thm:W}; for $W_M$ the threshold is $M=f$ by (I3); for $T_M(1,f)$ the exclusion $2M\le f$ of (I1) leaves, for example, $T_3(1,4)$ open.
\item \textbf{The $T$ half in general:} only the tile count and the glue; the converse of the glue is unknown.
\item \textbf{Nothing is claimed about the prime case or any integer outside $F$.}
\end{itemize}
\emph{Outside check.} The Lean statements and proofs were compared and audited inside this project only; no reader outside the project has checked them.

\subsection{Alternative to Step 1: the chain lemma, and certificates for (K)}\label{sub:chain}
\emph{Hand-proved, audited inside the project (verdict: sound, with four repairs, all applied here); not in Lean.} Step 1 above is a direct case analysis. A shorter argument replaces containment, separation and the area identity by one lemma (winding-number additivity; the integer frame is due to a study inside this project).
\begin{lemma}[chain lemma]\label{lem:chain}
Let $T$ and $\Pi_1,\dots,\Pi_k$ be triangles or quadrilaterals with counter-clockwise vertex lists, all with \emph{strictly} positive turns (cross products of consecutive edge vectors). (For polygons with five or more vertices positive turns do not imply convexity.) Then each $\Pi_i$ is a convex polygon: for a $3$- or $4$-gon whose exterior angles all lie in $(0,\pi)$ and sum to $2\pi$, the boundary is a simple closed convex curve. If, as formal sums of oriented segments, $\sum_i\partial\Pi_i=\partial T$, where the equality means equal oriented multiplicity on every line (collinear segments add: $[p,q]+[q,r]=[p,r]$ in any order of the points, and $[p,q]+[q,p]=0$), then the $\Pi_i$ have pairwise disjoint interiors, lie in $T$, and cover $T$.
\end{lemma}
\noindent\emph{Proof.} Winding numbers are additive in the boundary chain; a convex counter-clockwise polygon has winding number $1$ inside and $0$ outside, so $\sum_i\mathbf 1_{\Pi_i}=\mathbf 1_T$ off finitely many lines. If two interiors met, the sum would be $\ge2$ on an open set; if some $\Pi_i$ left $T$, it would be $\ge1$ off $T$. $\square$
(Caution: ``each piece inside $T$ and the areas add up'' does \emph{not} imply disjointness; and the vanishing of a sum of edge \emph{vectors} is not chain equality.)
\emph{The chain identity} holds for all integers $e,f,M,t,r,q$, because the points of Table~\ref{tab:pts} lie on five lines: $AB$ ($\xi=0$: $A,P_3,Q,B$), $OP$ ($y=0$: $O,P_2,C'$), $OB$ ($e\xi+fy=efbM$: $O,B,B'$), $\ell_t$ ($\varphi=efbt$: $P_2,Q,U_1$), $\ell_r$ ($\varphi=efbr$: $C',B',U_2,V_2$); in each the parameters cancel (for $U_1$: $f\cdot ef(ft-eq)+e\cdot ef(fq-et)=efb\,t$). Then on each line the oriented edges cancel: $C'\to O+O\to P_2+P_2\to C'=0$; $B\to Q+Q\to P_3+P_3\to A=B\to A$; $O\to B'+B'\to B=O\to B$; $P_2\to Q+Q\to U_1+U_1\to P_2=0$; $V_2\to B'+B'\to C'+C'\to U_2+U_2\to V_2=0$; and $P_2P_3$, $QV_2$, $U_1U_2$ are shared by two pieces with opposite orientation.
\emph{What remains} is strict positivity of every turn: each is a positive monomial times a product of the factors $t$, $r-t$, $eM-ft$, $fr-eM$, $fM-er$, $ft-eq$, $fq-er$, $fM-et$, $fq-et$. Five are independent: $t$, $C_3=eM-ft$, $C_4=fr-eM$, $C_6=fq-er$, $C_7=ft-eq$; the others follow from $f(r-t)=C_3+C_4$, $fq-et=C_6+e(r-t)$, $fM-et>0$ (as $et<eM<fM$) and the identity $e\,C_5=f\,C_3+f\,C_7+e\,C_6$ for $C_5=fM-er$ (and $C_5=u+1+fs$ below, positive anyway).
\emph{Certificates.} Put $\mu:=dn-e-1$ ($\ge0$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:equiv}), $\rho'=f-1-\rho\ge0$, $M=n+s$, so $\rho=es-dn$. For the canonical triple: $C_3=\rho+f\delta$, $C_4=\rho'+1$, $C_5=\mu+1+fs$, $C_6=\mu+1$, and $C_7=\mu+1+e$ if $\delta=0$; if $\delta=1$ ($M=fm'$, $n=em'$, $e-1=1+x$, $d-1=1+y$, $x,y\ge0$, $x+y\ge1$) then $C_7=(m'-1)ed+x+y+xy\ge1$. Each is a sum of non-negative terms with a positive constant. Checked exactly for all $982\,658$ admissible triples ($f\le120$, $M\le3f$); the $27\,322$ non-admissible triples in that range each violate a turn, so for these triples the canonical layout fails (compare Proposition~\ref{prop:exact}, which is the actual sharpness statement). The Lean proof of this paper does not use the chain lemma: it proves the placement conditions the older way (the sixteen named factors of Appendix~\ref{app:complex}, $143$ generated sign lemmas, kernel-checked). A Lean version of the chain lemma is planned, not done. Still needed for Step 2, unchanged: that each piece is a union of tiles (the edge-length identities of Table~\ref{tab:sides} and Proposition~\ref{prop:ladder}).

\subsection{The older proof: complex coordinates and cross-product tables}\label{app:complex}
The first version of this paper, and the Lean proof, use complex numbers with $O=0$ and $OB$ the positive real axis. With $D=4f^2-e^2$ write $\xi+i\sqrt D\,\zeta$ as $[\xi,\zeta]$ and put
\[
\varepsilon=e^{i\alpha}=\Bigl[\tfrac{\kappa}{2f^2},\tfrac{e}{2f^2}\Bigr],\quad
\eta=e^{i\beta}=\Bigl[\tfrac{e(3f^2-e^2)}{2f^3},\tfrac{b}{2f^3}\Bigr],\quad \omega=\overline{\varepsilon\eta}=e^{-i(\alpha+\beta)},
\]
with $\varepsilon^3\eta^2=-1$, $a+b\omega=c\bar\eta$, $f\omega+e\varepsilon=f\bar\eta$ (each checked by expansion). Then $P=fbM\varepsilon$ and the frame is $z=P-\xi\,\varepsilon+y\,\omega$: the first axis points from $P$ to $O$ (direction $-\varepsilon$) and the second from $P$ to $B$ (direction $\omega$). In these terms the points of Table~\ref{tab:pts} are $B=fbM$, $A=f^3M\varepsilon^2$, $P_2=b(fM-et)\varepsilon$, $P_3=P-eat\,\omega$, $Q=P+fbt\,\omega$, $R=P+fbr\,\omega$, $C'=bv\,\varepsilon$, $B'=cv$, $V_2=P+f^3t\,\bar\eta-ef^2r$, $X_4=P-fbq\,\varepsilon$, $U_1=P-ef^2t+ef^2q\,\bar\eta$, $U_2=P-ef^2r+ef^2q\,\bar\eta$ (exact check against the frame: block A).
The cross product $\langle u,v\rangle=\frac1{\sqrt D}\mathrm{Im}(\bar uv)$ is $1/\sqrt D$ times the usual one. The Lean proof shows that each cross product needed for the containment of a vertex in $OAB$, for the separation of two pieces and for the convexity of a piece equals a positive constant times a product of the \emph{named factors}
\begin{gather*}
C_2=r-t,\ C_3=eM-ft,\ C_3'=fM-et,\ C_4=fr-eM,\ C_5=fM-er,\\
C_6=fq-er,\ C_6'=fq-et,\ C_7=ft-eq,\ C_8=f^2t-e^2r,\ C_9=fr-eq,\\
C_{10}=M-q,\ E_1=bM+eft,\ E_2=eMf+bt,\ E_3=br+e^2t,\\
L_A=bfM-e\kappa t+f^3q,\ L_B=bfM-e\kappa r+f^3q,\ L_C=efbM-e^2\kappa r+f^4t,
\end{gather*}
and that these are positive for the canonical triple. Under (K) they all follow from the four inequalities: $C_3,C_4,C_6,C_7>0$ are (K) itself; $C_2>0$, $C_{10}>0$ and $C_3',C_5>0$ are (\ref{eq:consK}); $C_6'=C_6+eC_2$, $C_8=fC_7+eC_6$, $C_9=fC_2+C_7$ are sums of positive terms; $E_1,E_2,E_3$ are sums of products of positive numbers; $L_A,L_B,L_C$ express that $U_1$, $U_2$, $V_2$ lie on the segments $[P_2,Q]$, $[C',B']$ of Step 1, which is automatic (verified exactly: block E). The three tables (separation, containment, convexity; about $100$ rows) are generated by \texttt{python3 code/check\_thmW\_algebra.py 60 --tex tables.tex} and are not reproduced here (they are in the earlier draft in the repository history); each row is a bilinear expansion with the sine table $\langle1,\varepsilon\rangle=\frac e{2f^2}$, $\langle1,\omega\rangle=-\frac1{2f}$, $\langle1,\bar\eta\rangle=-\frac b{2f^3}$, $\langle\varepsilon,\omega\rangle=\langle\varepsilon,\bar\eta\rangle=-\frac1{2f}$, $\langle\omega,\bar\eta\rangle=\frac e{2f^2}$.
